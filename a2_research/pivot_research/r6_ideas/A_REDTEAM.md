# R6 Idea Gate Red-Team — A

**Disposition: all four directions FAIL-GATE as written.** This is a proposal review, not a run: I did not compute returns, signals, event counts, or any other outcomes, and did not access the sealed holdout. I checked the cited papers and source claims online and used only the submitted A.md and R5 report for project-history checks. “FAIL-GATE” means the idea cannot advance as written; it does not mean an outcome was measured or that a materially redesigned proposal could never be considered under a new preregistration.

## Summary and stricter ranking

The first idea is mostly a single-stock reversal factor rather than propagation among related assets. The second is the closest fit to the thesis but is substantially the failed R5 D4 mechanism with the sign chosen to match its observed negative result. The third has a serious five-minute-bar arithmetic error and a mismatch between bar counts and the proposed one-window-per-day signal. The fourth treats clustered stress dates as hundreds or thousands of independent events and mixes a 5% event assumption with an 80th-percentile VIX rule.

|---:|---|---|---:|---|
| 1 | Common-owner source pressure → peer reversal | **FAIL-GATE** | 2% | Near-repeat/sign reversal of D4, whose observed R5 direction was negative; common-owner graph density persists. |

## 1. Pressure-conditioned liquidity-provision reversal

**Verdict: FAIL-GATE.**

**(a) Papers — citations verified, proposed signal only partly supported.**

- [Campbell, Grossman & Wang (1993), *Trading Volume and Serial Correlation in Stock Returns*](https://doi.org/10.2307/2118454) exists. Its abstract says daily autocorrelation falls with volume and a high-volume price decline is more likely to raise expected returns than a low-volume decline. It motivates a volume-conditioned liquidity effect; it does not validate CLV × dollar volume as a signed-pressure measure or a five-day stock-level long–short rule.
- [Nagel (2012), *Evaporating Liquidity*](https://doi.org/10.1093/rfs/hhs066) exists and interprets short-term reversal returns as a proxy for liquidity-provision returns, which rise in market turmoil. It supports state dependence, not this signal or its post-2020 net returns.
- [Cox & Peterson (1994), *Stock Returns following Large One-Day Declines*](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-6261.1994.tb04428.x) exists. It attributes short-term reversal to bid–ask bounce and liquidity and finds no evidence consistent with overreaction; the authors also report poor longer-horizon performance after large declines. This is a warning about the alleged alpha, not clean support for a tradable five-day bounce.

**(b) Mega-cap survival.** The proposal itself says deep liquidity, tight spreads, competition and ETFs should reduce residual pressure. The conditional funding-stress argument is plausible but the design does not actually condition on stress; it proposes a daily signal. R5’s scope also demands pressure that persists, spreads or reverses through related assets. This idea forecasts each stock from its own candle and has no peer/network term. Even if statistically predictive, the current version does not clear thesis fit.

**(c) Power and cost.** The arithmetic in A’s rank-IC example is approximately right: with assumed daily IC SD 0.29 and 500 effective dates, an IC of 0.026 gives t≈2.0. But the 0.29 assumption is borrowed from R5 D4’s already observed series, not independently estimated for this different CLV-volume signal; it is not evidence that this signal has an adequately powered test. The assumed 500 effective dates and HAC structure are not justified by a design simulation. The 5% extreme-event counts (7,500/37,500 before overlap for S&P 100/500) are arithmetically right under that assumed event rate, but five-session overlapping outcomes and same-date market shocks make raw events much less informative than independent observations. Most importantly, A gives no rebalance rule or expected turnover. At 100% one-way turnover per day, 5 bp on each side is roughly 10 bp of round-trip trading cost per day (about 25% annualized before borrow/impact under that convention); an IC hurdle does not show the gross spread can pay this bill. High turnover is a likely gate failure, not a footnote.

**(d) Data feasibility.** Daily Dow OHLCV being present makes a candle proxy constructible, but it does not make CLV × volume equivalent to buyer-initiated order flow. Broad point-in-time S&P 100/500 rosters and prices remain unconfirmed in A; those inputs are necessary for the stated power plan. A’s local-header check is a reasonable first availability screen, not a broad-universe coverage pull.

**(e) Novelty.** The ingredients and mechanism are established short-term reversal/liquidity provision. Replacing a return with CLV × volume and selecting a five-session horizon is a measurement adaptation, not a substantially new network-pressure direction. There is no link definition, placebo graph, or peer-specific incremental test in this candidate.

## 2. Common-owner source pressure followed by peer reversal

**Verdict: FAIL-GATE.** This one is closest to the thesis, but the proposal is too close to the prior failed test to count as a fresh idea without stronger separation.

**(a) Papers — citations verified, effect sizes and conditions matter.**

- [Antón & Polk (2014), *Connected Stocks*](https://doi.org/10.1111/jofi.12149) exists. They connect stocks using common active mutual-fund owners, find excess return comovement, and propose a cross-stock reversal strategy. A source/peer reversal mechanism is directly related. Their sample covers thousands of stock pairs over 1980–2008; the paper’s trading result does not establish that a 30-stock Dow graph is identifiable or remains profitable after this project’s costs.
- [Coval & Stafford (2007), *Asset Fire Sales (and Purchases) in Equity Markets*](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jfineco.2006.09.007) exists. It shows flow-driven mutual-fund transactions create price pressure in overlapping holdings, and liquidity providers earn returns against constrained funds. This is compelling pressure evidence, but actual fund-flow-induced transactions are not the same thing as a daily signed candle at one holding.

**(b) Mega-cap survival.** A argues that source-specific shocks and leave-one-out peers solve D4’s graph-density problem; that is not demonstrated. R5 says the corrected common-owner graph was nearly complete in 24 of 25 quarters, so a 30-name peer average approximated the market. A adds no pre-outcome evidence that a directional source graph has materially more identifying variation. R5 D4 already tested five-session common-owner CLV/NCP and observed the opposite of its preregistered positive direction. The R6 rules permit the D4 reversal to return only as a **new** preregistration, but this specification remains very close to that same graph, pressure measure and five-session horizon, with the direction selected after the earlier result. Calling the old result “context only” does not make the redesign independent. This is the clearest hindsight/rescue risk in the set.

**(c) Power and cost.** The common rank-IC threshold inherits the same unsupported 0.29 SD assumption and 500 effective dates as idea 1. The event calculation is not reliable: 2% of stock-days gives 3,000/15,000 raw source shocks in S&P 100/500, but halving these to 1,500/7,500 does not account for clustering by date, source issuer, shared target, and overlapping five-day return windows. An event sample with 7,500 effective independent observations from 1,500 dates is not plausible without unusually weak cross-sectional dependence. Under two-way date/issuer clustering, effective information could be closer to a few hundred independent clusters; with the assumed 4% five-day residual-CAR SD, 2.8×0.04/√N is roughly 45 bp at N=625 and 112 bp at N=100. The paper offers no defensible path from raw counts to the assumed N_eff. No turnover or cost break-even is supplied; filing-date network signals are quarterly and might turnover less, but the daily source-pressure sleeve could still churn. A must disclose the trade rule before claiming a cost advantage.

**(d) Data feasibility.** R4’s assembled Dow-only 13F panel is relevant evidence that some inputs exist. It does not establish broad S&P 100/500 filing coverage, point-in-time identifier quality, or the independent event counts needed here. R5 also disclosed earlier CUSIP/ticker mapping and units defects in D4 inputs before correction. The current preflight needs at minimum a manifest showing point-in-time links, graph density, mapped securities and source-shock-event counts at Dow and broad-universe levels. Data status is partial, not passed for the proposed S&P mechanism panel.

**(e) Novelty.** The paper’s connected-return reversal strategy is almost exactly the family being proposed. Leave-one-out source attribution and a signed CLV shock are useful clarifications, but not yet a new mechanism. It also repeats the previously failed D4 test with a direction informed by that failure. This is not sufficiently distinct for an idea gate unless a genuinely new source of exogenous pressure or a sharply different link/timing design is specified before outcomes.

## 3. Intraday order-imbalance exhaustion and next-session reversal

**Verdict: FAIL-GATE.** The raw input might be obtainable through UNSW, but the proposed horizon and power claims are not supported as written.

**(a) Papers — existence verified; several claims are overstretched.**

- [Heston, Korajczyk & Sadka (2010), *Intraday Patterns in the Cross-section of Stock Returns*](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-6261.2010.01573.x) exists. It reports short-term reversals driven by temporary liquidity imbalances lasting less than an hour and bid–ask bounce. That is evidence for intraday reversals, not a next-session forecast from a fixed late-day imbalance.
- [Chordia, Roll & Subrahmanyam (2002), *Order Imbalance, Liquidity, and Market Returns*](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0304-405X(02)00136-8) exists. It documents market-wide returns after aggregate NYSE order imbalance, including reversal after high-negative-imbalance/large-negative-return days. That does not establish a cross-sectional stock-peer strategy among 30 names.
- [Rif & Utz (2021), *Short-term stock price reversals after extreme downward price movements*](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.qref.2021.05.004) exists. It finds 31% of an extreme negative one-minute return reverses in the next trading minute in Nasdaq-100 data, with larger reversals among large/liquid names. This does not validate a move from a single 30-minute late-session signal to next-session returns; the cited effect is on the following minute and is especially exposed to spread and execution effects.

**(b) Mega-cap survival.** Mega-caps are in the microstructure literature, but efficient electronic markets make a next-session reversal much harder to monetize than a subsequent-minute bounce. The studies cited are mostly pre-2020 or use a very different horizon; the idea supplies no evidence the effect survived post-2020 competition. A next-session horizon could avoid some bid–ask bounce, but that is a new hypothesis and the citation set does not establish its sign or magnitude.

**(c) Power and costs.** The five-minute-bar counts are wrong by a factor of five. At 78 five-minute bars per regular session, 1,500 sessions, and 100/500 stocks, the totals are **11.7 million** bars for S&P 100 and **58.5 million** for S&P 500, not 58.5/292.5 million. At the assumed 1-in-1,000 bar event rate, raw counts would be 11,700/58,500; a 1% effective fraction gives 117/585, not 585/2,925. With assumed 2% next-session abnormal-return SD, the planning threshold for 80% probability of observing t≥2 is about 52/23 bp at those effective counts, before date/issuer clustering. More fundamentally, the candidate defines one 30-minute feature per stock-day, while its event count treats each of the 78 five-minute bars as an independent opportunity; the count does not match the proposed signal. The cited paper’s one-in-1,000 event is an extreme **one-minute return**, not an extreme late-session order-imbalance window. The usable event count is therefore unknown. The 0.018–0.026 rank-IC hurdle does not rescue this event design, and a 23–52 bp next-session effect has to survive 5 bp/side costs, spread, impact, shorting and auction timing. No post-cost power is shown.

**(d) Data feasibility.** A claims FactSet intraday trades/quotes are the likely source. UNSW’s public [Business School research database catalogue](https://www.unsw.edu.au/business/our-research/research-environment/databases) actually lists NYSE TAQ/WRDS with intraday trades and quotes from 1993 onward, but access is through WRDS premium or contact with the data administrator; ordinary students may not receive individual WRDS accounts. No local intraday file was identified in A. This is a plausible source, not a completed coverage pull or confirmation the student team can retrieve the fields. Also, TAQ is not automatically a signed order-imbalance series: the trade classification method, quote alignment, corporate actions and exact covered names must be validated.

**(e) Novelty.** The pressure signal is a new window choice over a well-known order-imbalance/reversal family, but the cited literature already reports sub-hour liquidity-imbalance reversals and one-minute extreme reversals. Extending that effect to next-session Dow returns is a testable adaptation, not clear conceptual novelty; because the literature horizon does not match, it is currently an unsupported horizon extrapolation.

## 4. Funding-stress liquidity spillover and reversal in related stocks

**Verdict: FAIL-GATE.** The liquidity mechanism is credible in general, but the proposed peer edge and its event power are not established.

**(a) Papers — citations verified, claims need narrower wording.**

- [Hameed, Kang & Viswanathan (2010), *Stock Market Declines and Liquidity*](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-6261.2009.01529.x) exists. It finds negative market returns reduce stock liquidity, especially under tight funding, reports interindustry liquidity spillovers, and finds returns to supplying liquidity after large drops. It supports a market/interindustry funding-liquidity channel; it does not show that a GICS peer-pressure score predicts returns across Dow stocks.
- [Nagel (2012), *Evaporating Liquidity*](https://doi.org/10.1093/rfs/hhs066) exists and reports short-term reversal/liquidity-provision returns and conditional Sharpe ratios rise during turmoil. It supports the stress-conditioned motivation, not the proposed GICS-specific spread or net strategy.

**(b) Mega-cap survival.** The paper-supported premise is that liquidity supply weakens in funding stress. The proposal then assumes this becomes a cross-sectional peer effect in large Dow stocks, but does not explain why GICS membership identifies the stocks jointly constrained or why the move is not a common market beta shock. R5 D5’s thin SIC groups are a warning against assuming industry groups provide enough independent peers; GICS may be broader, but no group counts or network variation are supplied. In stress, all Dow names can move together, leaving little market-neutral edge after residualization.

**(c) Power and costs.** The claimed 3,750 raw events assumes 1,500 dates × 500 stocks × 5% stress dates × 10% qualifying stocks. But “VIX above its trailing 80th percentile” implies roughly 20% of dates by construction, not 5%; if 5% is intended, the threshold must be separately defined and frozen. Either way, the dominant issue is clustering: 5% of 1,500 dates is only 75 stress dates, and 20% is 300 dates. The 1,875 N_eff assumes 50% of all stock-date cells are independent, although pressure events co-occur on the same stress date and across related names. Date/issuer two-way clustering and persistence in stress episodes reduce effective information sharply. As an intentionally conservative date-cluster illustration, with 75 independent stress dates and 4% five-day return SD, the 80%-power effect needed to observe t≥2 is about 2.84×4%/√75 ≈ **1.31%**; even 300 independent dates imply about **65 bp**. If the target effect is 26 bp, it would require about `(2.84×0.04/0.0026)^2 ≈ 1,910` independent clusters, far more than the stress-date count. Broad cross-sectional breadth can improve precision only to the extent residual returns are independent within dates; the proposal has no such variance decomposition. Gross return forecasts also need to exceed costs and preserve the ≤50% turnover or cost-to-gross rule, neither of which is estimated.

**(d) Data feasibility.** Daily Dow OHLCV and an external VIX series are plausible. The required point-in-time S&P 500 roster and GICS membership are unconfirmed in A; nor is the suggested “count stress dates” feasibility step reported as completed. The Dow-only first-stage check is possible, but it would not establish power of the broad panel used in the proposal. Gate (d) remains open.

**(e) Novelty.** Stress-conditioned liquidity provision and industry liquidity spillover are established results. Replacing industry returns with CLV × volume in GICS groups is not enough novelty, especially when idea 1 uses the same proxy and idea 4 has no newly specified causal link beyond group membership. A genuinely new test would need an ex-ante, non-generic network exposure and demonstrate incremental prediction beyond the market and group response.

## Coordinator recommendation

Do not send any of these four ideas to portfolio implementation as written. If one must remain in the R6 backlog, retain #2 only as a **new, explicitly labeled D4-reversal preregistration candidate**, with a red-team rule that disqualifies it unless an outcome-free coverage test demonstrates non-saturated directional links and a materially different estimand from R5 D4. Do not use an outcome sign from R5 as confirmatory evidence. Otherwise, return to the idea gate with a genuinely new source of pressure or network and require (i) real sample-row feasibility before writing a preregistration, (ii) two-way date/issuer power simulation, and (iii) a turnover/cost bound tied to the precise holding and rebalance rule. No direction passes this review.
