# R6 Idea Gate — E: monthly peer pressure and continuation

**Scope.** Three monthly designs that ask whether positive pressure persists within an industry, is anchored by peer 52-week highs, or crosses explicit customer–supplier links. Signal estimation is proposed on a point-in-time S&P 500 panel; portfolio implementation is on the Dow 30. This is a literature and power design only: no project prices, returns, signals, or outcomes were loaded or calculated, and no holdout data were used.

## Ranking and decision

| Rank | Direction | Gate view | Subjective chance of clearing the full R6 stack |
|---:|---|---|---:|
| 1 | Industry peer buying-pressure diffusion | **FAIL-GATE for testing now** | 4% |
| 2 | Customer–supplier pressure / connected-firm momentum | **FAIL-GATE for testing now** | 2% |
| 3 | Peer 52-week-high anchoring with pressure confirmation | **FAIL-GATE for testing now** | 1% |

These are judgmental chances of passing the entire R6 process, not estimated probabilities. The mechanisms are literature-motivated, but the design-matched power is poor for the 30-stock trading panel and the evidence does not establish survival in Dow-sized firms after 2010. The connected-firm idea has especially weak free-link coverage evidence for Dow names. None should proceed to outcomes without a separate, outcome-blind data and power decision.

## Common monthly implementation and cost hurdle

The common portfolio proposal is monthly top-five-of-Dow long-only, benchmarked against EW30, with a **20% total-notional turnover cap per monthly rebalance** (buys plus sells divided by NAV, relative to EW30). Names are retained unless a new candidate crosses a fixed score buffer; no full replacement is assumed. The feasibility of maintaining this cap is unknown until a preregistered turnover calculation, and it cannot be claimed from the idea stage.

I called `r4_harness.phase3.break_even_rank_ic` with monthly turnover `0.20`, assumed one-month cross-sectional return dispersion `0.06`, cost `0.0005` per side, and top fraction `5/30`. The cost-only hurdle is **Spearman rank IC = 0.001155**. This is an assumption-based conversion, not a project-data estimate. It excludes spread/impact beyond the stipulated 5 bp per side and is much lower than the IC needed for a statistically powered test.

### Design-matched power

I ran `r4_harness/power_sim.py` with `horizon=21`, AR(1) signal autocorrelation `0.20`, 2,000 simulations, and a **one-sided Newey–West t ≥ 2** criterion. The monthly design has approximately **71 non-overlapping 21-session forecast dates** over the stated six-year development window; the S&P500 and Dow rows below use those same dates. At a planted rank IC of `0.010` (about 8.7 times the cost hurdle), estimated power is only **48.5%** with 500 assets and **13.9%** with 30 assets. The 80%-power detectable ICs are **0.01831** and **0.07593**, respectively; HAC effective N is 48.3 dates. Because industry and customer–supplier links create within-group dependence, these cross-sectional calculations are optimistic for all three designs, especially the connected-network designs. `power_sim.py` is a synthetic design calculator; the dates and IC are assumptions, not observed outcomes.

For context only, treating all 1,479 development trading days as separate overlapping observations at the same 21-day horizon produces optimistic 80%-power MDEs of **0.00401 (S&P500)** and **0.01664 (Dow30)** and power of **100.0%** and **39.4%** at IC 0.010. That is not the proposed monthly sampling plan and does not cure the Dow power problem. No separate variant or parameter sweep is created by showing this cadence comparison.

## 1. Industry peer buying-pressure diffusion

**Question.** Does measured positive buying pressure in a firm predict later returns among its industry peers, at a monthly horizon, beyond each target firm's own pressure?

**(a) Literature.** Moskowitz and Grinblatt, [“Do Industries Explain Momentum?” *Journal of Finance* (1999), DOI 10.1111/0022-1082.00146](https://doi.org/10.1111/0022-1082.00146), document profitable industry momentum over intermediate horizons and show industry effects explain much individual-stock momentum. Hou, [“Industry Information Diffusion and the Lead-lag Effect in Stock Returns,” *Review of Financial Studies* (2007), DOI 10.1093/rfs/hhm003](https://doi.org/10.1093/rfs/hhm003), finds large-firm/ small-firm lead–lag is predominantly intra-industry and interprets it as sluggish information adjustment. These papers support group-linked continuation, but use return-based industry signals rather than a direct measure of order-flow pressure.

**(b) Mega-cap, post-2010 case.** A plausible case is that Dow firms remain exposed to common industry information and institutional attention does not eliminate slow adjustment to all peer-specific shocks. The counterevidence is material: Hou reports stronger effects in small, less competitive, and neglected industries, which is the opposite of a strong mega-cap survival case. A 2023 review by Jegadeesh and Titman, [“Momentum: Evidence and insights 30 years later,” DOI 10.1016/j.pacfin.2023.102202](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.pacfin.2023.102202), reports post-2000 momentum across many markets but not support for it in the U.S. market. The cited industry papers do not prove that Dow-scale **pressure diffusion** persisted after 2010; survival remains a weak hypothesis to test, not a fact.

**(c) Power.** Shared design calculation above: at monthly 21-session cadence, `n_assets=500`, `n_dates=71`, `horizon=21`, `rho=.20`, `mean_ic=.010` yields MDE80 `.01831` and power `.4845`; `n_assets=30` yields MDE80 `.07593` and power `.1385`. A peer network partitions the S&P500 into industries, so the nominal 500-asset calculation overstates independent breadth. The cost hurdle is `.001155`; satisfying costs alone is not remotely enough for t≥2 in the Dow sample.

**(d) Exact free data.** Daily OHLCV for S&P500 estimation names and Dow trading names, point-in-time S&P500 constituents, security identifiers, historical industry codes, and corporate-action adjustments. Prices can be sourced from a free public price archive, but point-in-time constituent and SIC histories require careful reconstruction from public archived S&P constituent lists and SEC filings; neither current constituent lists nor current SIC assignments are acceptable substitutes. The Fama–French Data Library provides free 49-industry definitions/portfolio series, and SEC EDGAR submissions and company filings are free ([French industry classification notes](https://mba.tuck.dartmouth.edu/pages/faculty/ken.french/Data_Library/changes_ind.html); [SEC public data and filings](https://www.sec.gov/about/webmaster-frequently-asked-questions)). No FactSet is proposed. A reproducible point-in-time constituent/SIC panel is not yet established, so this is a material free-data gate.

**(e) Novelty.** Instead of buying stocks based on their own trailing industry returns, aggregate signed daily volume and close-location value (CLV) over the prior 21 sessions for each source firm, exclude the target, and use the industry peer pressure aggregate as the target's sole primary score for next-month returns. The frozen test would ask for positive peer-pressure IC after controlling for own-stock pressure, and compare against shuffled within-industry links. This directly tests pressure transmission; it is not the same signal or horizon as the classic 6–12 month industry-return strategy.

## 2. Customer–supplier pressure and connected-firm momentum

**Question.** Does a customer's positive pressure/market response predict later supplier returns through a disclosed operating link?

**(a) Literature.** Cohen and Frazzini, [“Economic Links and Predictable Returns,” *Journal of Finance* (2008), DOI 10.1111/j.1540-6261.2008.01379.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-6261.2008.01379.x), find that customer-firm news predicts supplier returns, consistent with slow diffusion across economically linked firms. A recent replication/update, [“Customer Momentum” (2023), arXiv:2301.11394](https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.11394), reports the customer-momentum relation but also says it appears to have declined after discovery. The latter is a working paper and should be treated as replication evidence, not definitive proof of a current premium.

**(b) Mega-cap, post-2010 case.** Dow firms often have extensive supplier/customer networks, so an economically important linked shock could remain material even when both firms are large. But the link's existence alone does not imply inattentive pricing; large institutions may follow the chain promptly. The 2023 update's decline-after-discovery finding directly weakens persistence. More importantly, R5's outcome-blind feasibility review found only **3 listed customer–supplier pairs, all with Amgen as supplier, zero Dow customer pairs, and zero earnings events** in its fixed sample (`R5_FINAL.md`, section 5 / `r5_D7/LINK_QA.md`). That is not a coverage case for a Dow trade. No verified paper establishes post-2010 customer–supplier **pressure** continuation in mega-caps.

**(c) Power.** The shared S&P500 monthly 21-day scenario gives 48.5% power at IC .010 and MDE80 .01831 before link clustering; the Dow case gives 13.9% and MDE80 .07593. For this design, usable observations are dyads but independent shock dates are the true limiting units. The 71-date synthetic run assumes 500 usable assets on every date and therefore overstates power when only a subset have disclosed links. The cost hurdle remains .001155 at the common turnover assumption.

**(d) Exact free data.** Point-in-time S&P500/Dow identities and adjusted daily OHLCV; SEC EDGAR 10-K filings to extract explicitly disclosed major customer/supplier identities, link direction, filing date, and the first public availability date. SEC filing content is free and reusable, but relationship extraction is largely unstructured, reporting is selective, and many firms do not disclose a named customer. The previous Dow-only free-link check is a severe warning. No paid FactSet customer–supplier panel is allowed under this idea. **Free in principle, not yet buildable at adequate Dow coverage.**

**(e) Novelty.** Cohen–Frazzini use customer returns and principal-customer links; this proposal tests customer *buying pressure* (signed volume/CLV) as the signal and requires a timestamped public filing before treating a link as known. The primary hypothesis is positive next-month supplier response to positive customer pressure. This distinguishes information diffusion from simultaneous industry co-movement, while explicitly requiring a free-link coverage gate before any outcome access.

## 3. Peer 52-week-high anchoring, confirmed by peer pressure

**Question.** Do industry peers near their own 52-week highs, when accompanied by positive peer buying pressure, predict continuation in a connected target stock?

**(a) Literature.** George and Hwang, [“The 52-Week High and Momentum Investing,” *Journal of Finance* (2004), DOI 10.1111/j.1540-6261.2004.00695.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-6261.2004.00695.x), show that price nearness to a stock's own 52-week high explains a substantial part of momentum profits. Hong, Jordan, and Liu, [“Industry Information and the 52-Week High Effect,” *Pacific-Basin Finance Journal* (2015), DOI 10.1016/j.pacfin.2015.02.011](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.pacfin.2015.02.011), find an industry-level 52-week-high effect and argue it is consistent with underreaction to industry information rather than only firm-specific anchoring. Their U.S. sample runs through 2009, and the effect is stronger for small, young, hard-to-value, low-coverage firms—limiting direct application to Dow names.

**(b) Mega-cap, post-2010 case.** The mechanism may survive if investors continue to anchor on salient highs and interpret peer highs as gradual industry information; liquid mega-caps can still move on shared shocks. However, the published industry extension's stronger effects in less visible/smaller firms cut against this case, and its data end before 2010. The 2023 Jegadeesh–Titman review notes broad post-2000 momentum across markets but not in the U.S.; this is a broad caution rather than a direct test of peer 52-week-high signals. There is no verified evidence here that the peer extension survives among U.S. mega-caps after 2010.

**(c) Power.** At the same monthly 21-day cadence, 71 dates and assumed IC .010 yield power .4845 / MDE80 .01831 for 500 assets and power .1385 / MDE80 .07593 for 30 assets. Breadth is reduced because the peer statistic is shared within industries; this makes the 500-name result optimistic. A small cost hurdle (.001155) does not compensate for weak test power. The daily 1,479-date comparison is only an upper bound and not the monthly design.

**(d) Exact free data.** Point-in-time membership and adjusted daily highs/closes for S&P500-estimation and Dow-trading names; historical industry identifiers; corporate-action adjustment; free point-in-time constituents; and daily volume for the pressure confirmation. Public price archives and SEC filings can supply much of this, but historical index membership and historical industry mapping need reconstruction and quality control. Fama–French's SIC-based industry definitions are free. No FactSet data is proposed; absent a reliable archived roster and identifier map, the S&P500 exercise risks survivorship and cannot pass the data gate.

**(e) Novelty.** The score is the leave-one-out monthly industry breadth of firms whose close is near their own trailing 52-week high, multiplied by the industry's aggregate signed-volume/CLV pressure; the predicted sign is positive and horizon is the next 21 sessions. This is one fixed hybrid score, not a sweep of high windows or peer definitions. The mechanism test must show that peer-high breadth contributes beyond the target's own 52-week-high distance and peer pressure alone; a within-industry shuffled-peer placebo is fixed in advance. This adds an explicit measurable-pressure confirmation to published own-stock and industry high anchoring.

## Shared design constraints and open data checks

- Monthly formation dates only; next 21 trading sessions are the primary horizon. No daily/weekly variants are authorized by this idea memo.
- The point-in-time S&P500 panel is for estimating the mechanism and power; a positive result in the S&P500 does not substitute for the pre-specified Dow test.
- All three use the common top-5 Dow / EW30 benchmark and 20% total-turnover target. If measured turnover breaches the R6 50% cap, the direction fails; the cap cannot be used to suppress the reported actual turnover.
- Public sources are free, but “free” does not mean complete: current S&P500 membership is not a valid historical roster, SEC customer disclosures are sparse, and current industry codes can leak future classifications.
- This memo does not authorize a variant. Any selected direction still needs a frozen preregistration, a clear point-in-time input manifest, enough independent monthly dates/events, and a coordinator decision before any outcome test.
