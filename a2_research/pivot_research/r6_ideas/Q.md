# Round Q — Can long-short salvage an untried pressure mechanism?

## Decision: none is plausible enough to advance

**Do not open a new variant or outcome test.** A long-short portfolio can reduce market beta and may improve its own Sharpe ratio, but the fixed criterion still requires (i) positive return relative to EW30 and (ii) a Sharpe above EW30's roughly 0.9, plus a family-adjusted significant excess-return test. For a market-neutral book, this is a particularly demanding combination: low volatility can raise its standalone Sharpe, but does not make its mean return exceed the Dow benchmark. The math below shows that a genuinely competitive monthly long-short signal would need an unusually large rank IC; no untried free-data pressure mechanism in the R5/R6 record has a documented post-2020 mega-cap effect of that scale.

This is a decision-file and planning-math review only. I read no prices, returns, ICs, strategy outcomes or holdout data, and ran no outcome or signal analysis.

## Hurdle math

For an equal-weight long-short rank portfolio, use the fundamental law approximation `IR ≈ IC × sqrt(B)`, where `B` is the number of independent active bets per year and IC is the cross-sectional rank IC. It is an idealization: overlapping positions, serial correlation, sector clustering and implementation costs reduce effective breadth and increase the required IC.

1. **Sharpe comparison alone.** At monthly formation/holding, the optimistic breadth is only `B=12`. To achieve Sharpe 0.9 requires `IC ≥ 0.9/sqrt(12) = 0.260`. With six years of non-overlapping monthly returns, a one-sided normal approximation gives `t ≈ SR × sqrt(6)`. A raw p around 0.006 (needed for a plausible Holm pass in this nine-endpoint family) requires `SR ≈ 2.50/sqrt(6) = 1.02`, or `IC ≈ 1.02/sqrt(12) = 0.294`, before any serial-dependence or cluster penalty.

2. **The benchmark-return hurdle makes a low-volatility market-neutral book harder.** Let `σ_EW` be EW30 volatility and `σ_LS` the long-short volatility. Since EW30's Sharpe is about 0.9, its mean is about `0.9 × σ_EW`. To have positive return over EW30, a long-short strategy needs `μ_LS > 0.9 × σ_EW`, or standalone `SR_LS > 0.9 × (σ_EW/σ_LS)`. If the market-neutral book has half the benchmark's volatility, the return comparison alone implies `SR_LS > 1.8` and, at monthly breadth, `IC > 1.8/sqrt(12) = 0.520`. A lower volatility does not reduce this return hurdle; it raises the standalone Sharpe needed to clear it.

3. **Holm excess-return test.** The frozen R6 manifest contains nine endpoints, including already-small raw p-values H2=0.001996 and H1=0.028195. If one new result is the next-smallest and clears the H2/H1 ordering, its raw p must be about `≤0.00625` to pass the next Holm step (`0.05/8`); being the smallest would require `≤0.05/9=0.00556`. Thus the `SR≈1.02` / `IC≈0.294` calculation above is a favorable minimum for testing a zero-mean active return, not a relaxation of the benchmark-return condition. The exact adjusted p depends on the other frozen and future family values.

Daily rebalancing could nominally offer `B≈252`, reducing the IC needed for 0.9 Sharpe to `0.9/sqrt(252)=0.057` (or about `1.02/sqrt(252)=0.064` for the six-year Holm-significance approximation). But it does not rescue the market-neutral comparison: costs at 50% total turnover each day are 5 bp × 0.50 = 2.5 bp of NAV per day, or about 6.3% simple annual cost. The goal's alternative cost rule would require gross edge above four times that cost, roughly 25% per year at that turnover. If monthly rebalancing is used to control costs, the relevant required IC returns to roughly 0.29–0.52 under the assumptions above.

These equations are planning bounds, not claims about an achieved effect. The benchmark Sharpe and nine-endpoint family size come from the fixed R6 criterion and coordinator decisions; the volatility ratio example is explicitly illustrative. A real Holm block-bootstrap threshold depends on the family p-values and actual return-path dependence, neither of which can be inferred from a hypothetical Sharpe alone.

## Gates (a)–(e): why no candidate passes the pre-screen

**(a) Literature and effect size — FAIL.** The reviewed R5/R6 literature documents mechanism-specific effects, but no untried, free-data pressure channel with a comparable post-2020 mega-cap long-short Sharpe or monthly rank IC near 0.29, much less the roughly 0.52 return-comparison hurdle in the lower-risk example. Published abnormal returns, overnight-only components, event reactions, ownership elasticities and market-wide ETF effects are not interchangeable with net Dow long-short Sharpe. Earlier R6 decisions conclude that no screened free-data effect met the criterion-4 feasibility bar; the new long-short framing does not produce a missing effect-size estimate.

**(b) Large-cap and post-2020 survival — FAIL.** The strongest reported results for related channels mostly use broad historical panels, older samples, different horizons, or returns before costs. A long-short construction does not establish that a pressure premium survives in 30 current mega-caps after 2020; in a liquid universe, the short leg also incurs borrow and turnover frictions that are not captured by stock-trading commissions alone. No evidence available in the staged R5/R6 research shows a sufficiently large persistent post-2020 premium in the eligible Dow universe.

**(c) Power and breadth — FAIL.** Thirty names permit a top/bottom five sort, but a month offers one cross-sectional decision and only about 72 monthly observations across six years. Monthly breadth is at most 12 independent portfolio bets per year, before autocorrelation and cluster dependence; the expected IC requirements above are far beyond the planning effects used in earlier R6 screening. Moving the mechanism test to the S&P 500 can improve IC measurement precision, but criterion 4 is ultimately about the Dow portfolio and cannot be satisfied by S&P-only power.

**(d) Data and costs — FAIL.** Free/staged inputs have already enabled the tested or gated families: daily OHLCV and overnight components; SIC industry links; common-owner 13F; SEC insider and customer disclosures; FINRA short volume/interest; ETF holdings/flows; earnings clocks; and public ownership disclosures. Other plausible high-frequency pressure measures such as order-book OFI require paid ITCH/LOBSTER data, while passive mandate decomposition and historical institution classifications are not present in the free 13F panel. Lower-turnover monthly execution reduces explicit commission drag, but does not create the missing alpha; a daily approach faces material 0.05%-per-side costs under the fixed turnover rule.

**(e) Novelty and non-rescue — FAIL for any proposed substitute.** The live and screened pressure ideas already cover own-stock overnight momentum (P1), pairs/co-movement divergence (P2), abnormal-volume attention (P3), passive ownership (P4), common ownership, leader-follower industry links, insider buying, ETF transmission, short interest/volume, earnings and event/network mechanisms. Recasting one as top-K-minus-bottom-K would be a portfolio wrapper around a tested signal, not a new pressure mechanism. Combining failed or already-known signals is expressly rejected by decision 86 because it would use observed development outcomes for selection. A remaining signal would need a new, pre-outcome data source or identification strategy and a documented effect-size case; none is currently evidenced.

## Disposition

**No long-short candidate is recommended.** A market-neutral portfolio could have lower variance than EW30, but criterion 4 still demands more return than EW30 and a Sharpe above about 0.9. The monthly breadth math implies at least a roughly 0.29 rank IC for a favorable zero-mean Holm test and roughly 0.52 if the long-short volatility is half EW30's just to clear the mean-return comparison; real dependence, costs and borrow push these hurdles higher. The reasonable action is to wait for genuinely new data or a user-authorized change to the fixed criterion, not to create another signal variant from the exhausted free-data families.
