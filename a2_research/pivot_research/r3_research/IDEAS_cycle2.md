## Shared implementation and selection protocol

- **Data and signal timing:** Use adjusted close-to-close simple returns, adjusted daily OHLC and daily share volume from the point-in-time Dow roster or fixed cross-asset basket. For dollar volume use adjusted close times volume. Compute each signal after close using completed observations through that close; trade at the next eligible session's open. Require every stated lookback observation; do not shorten a window or carry a missing observation forward. A zero/nonpositive dollar-volume observation or an insufficient downside sample makes that candidate-universe run invalid and must be disclosed, not imputed.
- **Portfolio weights:** Long-only, fully invested across all 30 names, zero cash. Rank the specified score in ascending order, with average ranks for ties. Set `r_i=(rank_i-1)/29` and `w_i=(1/30)*(1+0.8*(2*r_i-1))`. Thus a lower score receives a lower weight, each target lies between 0.67% and 6.00% absent ties, and weights sum to 100%. Use the full-universe tilt; no top-five/top-15 book, leverage or shorting.
- **Rebalance and no-trade band:** Rebalance every 21 eligible sessions on the validated engine's schedule anchored at 2020-11-27. At each scheduled rebalance, compare targets to drifted pre-trade weights. If the sum of absolute weight changes is below 1.0% of pre-trade NAV, skip the entire rebalance; otherwise reset all 30 weights to target. Charge 0.05% per side on actual traded notional, including initial investment and terminal liquidation. EW30 uses the same dates, fills and costs.
- **Selection, frozen separately by family:** Before inspecting any results, record this rule. For each of the three families, run its two variants on both registered selection samples, Dow and cross-asset, 2020-11-27 through 2022-12-30. Exclude a variant if mean actual traded notional exceeds 50% of pre-trade NAV per rebalance in either selection universe. Of the remaining variants **within that family**, freeze the one with the highest unweighted mean annualized daily net Sharpe across the two universes. Break ties by lower two-universe average turnover, then the shorter lookback or less extreme threshold listed first. If neither qualifies, freeze EW30 for that family. Only after writing the three frozen choices may the implementer run each frozen family once in the four fixed development test windows. Do not retune on any test result. This permits up to three distinct directions to be evaluated while preserving selection-only choice; all six unique candidate variants count toward the trial inventory.
- **Evaluation:** For each frozen family report net return, Sharpe, max drawdown, information ratio and annualized tracking error versus same-universe EW30, actual turnover, active share, pooled block-bootstrap p-value and deflated Sharpe using the cumulative trial count. The rank tilt has target active share close to 20.7% before band skips; verify achieved test-period active share and tracking error. Expected TE ranges below are planning estimates, not measured values. State which criteria fail. A development pass still requires the coordinator's sealed-holdout test.

## Idea 1 — Amihud illiquidity premium, sized modestly

**Economic rationale.** Amihud (2002) finds a positive relation between illiquidity and expected stock returns and defines a daily-data illiquidity proxy as absolute return divided by dollar volume. This is a direct, testable return hypothesis rather than another low-volume reversal: the score uses no sign or prior price reversal. It may generalize imperfectly to broad ETFs, and the published premium is compensation for illiquidity, so this candidate deliberately exposes the trade-off between that premium and actual implementation costs.

**Exact score.** For each asset and each daily interval `d` in the last `L` completed intervals, compute `ILLIQ_d = abs(r_d) / (adjusted_close_d * volume_d)`. The score is the arithmetic mean of these `L` values (scale by 1,000,000 for reporting only; ranking is unchanged). Rank **descending** on the score, so higher estimated illiquidity receives higher portfolio rank/weight. Do not use signed returns, dollar-volume trends or a volume filter. Use the 21-session portfolio rebalance and common 1% no-trade band.

**Variants and selection.** `ILLIQ21_a08` (`L=21`) and `ILLIQ63_a08` (`L=63`); select between these within the family using the shared selection rule. The 63-session estimate should be more stable, while 21 sessions tests whether a faster-moving premium is visible; no third lookback is authorized.

**Expected active risk, turnover and cost survival.** Target active share is about 20.7% and expected annualized TE is roughly 3–8%. A 21/63-day rolling score with 21-session rebalancing should produce around 10–35% traded notional per rebalance, with the 63-day variant nearer the low end. Cost drag under the flat 5 bp model should remain manageable if turnover is below the registered 50% ceiling. Dollar volume is observable for both stock and ETF baskets, though its economic meaning differs across them; actual ETF and stock spreads are not observed, so the fixed cost model is only a coarse check.

**Known failure mode.** The illiquidity premium may be absent in these large-cap stocks and liquid ETFs, or may be more than offset by bid-ask spread, market impact, taxes and ETF roll or tracking costs not represented by 5 bp per side. Dollar volume can rise mechanically during volatile periods, making Amihud rankings unstable. A failed candidate would not show that illiquidity is irrelevant in markets with realistic costs.

**Citation.** Amihud, Y. (2002), “Illiquidity and stock returns: cross-section and time-series effects,” *Journal of Financial Markets*, 5(1), 31–56. https://doi.org/10.1016/S1386-4181(01)00024-6.

## Idea 2 — Cross-sectional tilt by realized return skewness

**Economic rationale.** Asset payoffs with attractive right-tail outcomes can command high prices when investors value skewness. Harvey and Siddique (2000) formalize the role of conditional skewness in asset pricing tests. This is a distribution-shape statistic over the full sample, not cycle 1's MAX statistic based on the single largest daily return; it is nevertheless related to the same broad lottery-demand story and should not be treated as independent evidence.

**Exact score.** For each asset, over the last `L` completed close-to-close returns compute population central moments `m2 = mean((r-mean(r))^2)` and `m3 = mean((r-mean(r))^3)`, then `SKEW_L=m3/(m2**1.5)`. Require `m2>0`. Rank **ascending on SKEW_L**, giving lower or more negative realized skewness the higher weight; this direction is fixed to test whether high positive-skew assets are overpriced. Do not winsorize daily returns or adjust for market beta. Apply the common full-investment tilt, rebalance and cost rule.

**Variants and selection.** `SKEW42_a08` (`L=42`) and `SKEW63_a08` (`L=63`); select between these within the family using the shared selection rule. Both use a short, available history; 42 is the faster response and 63 the steadier estimate.

**Expected active risk, turnover and cost survival.** Expected target active share is about 20.7%, with annualized TE around 3–8%. Since a 42/63-day sample skew is noisy, expect 15–40% actual traded notional per 21-session rebalance, potentially toward the high end; the 1% no-trade band may suppress small changes. Monthly-ish trading and a diversified 30-name tilt can keep modeled costs below the 50% turnover limit, but realized spreads can still matter. The return-only calculation works on both asset classes, although the economic interpretation of skew differs between individual firms and diversified ETFs.

**Known failure mode.** Sample skewness is dominated by a few observations, especially with only 42 days; sorting on it can recreate unstable exposure to jumpy assets. Choosing low-skew names may load on crash risk, because investors may require a premium to hold negatively skewed assets. Harvey and Siddique study systematic/conditional skewness, whereas this simple signal is univariate realized skewness; the citation motivates a question, not this exact score.

**Citation.** Harvey, C. R. and Siddique, A. (2000), “Conditional Skewness in Asset Pricing Tests,” *The Journal of Finance*, 55(3), 1263–1295. https://doi.org/10.1111/0022-1082.00247.

## Idea 3 — Avoid assets with high downside market beta

**Economic rationale.** Ang, Chen and Xing (2006) find that stocks with greater sensitivity to market declines earn a downside-risk premium. A long-only investor may prefer low downside sensitivity for Sharpe and drawdown control, even if doing so gives up that premium. This is specifically conditional co-movement on down-market observations, not the 63-day all-observation beta already tested in the earlier candidate screen. The factor overlap is a known concern, especially after the inverse-heat study found a strong relation between its selected weights and low beta.

**Exact score.** For each asset `i`, on each date in the trailing 63 completed daily return observations compute a leave-one-out benchmark `m_i,d = mean(r_j,d for all roster assets j != i)`. Select the downside observations where `m_i,d < 0`; estimate an OLS regression with intercept `r_i,d = c_i + b_i_down*m_i,d + e_i,d` using only those observations. The score is the OLS slope `b_i_down`. Require at least 15 downside observations for every asset at a rebalance; otherwise that candidate-universe run fails. Rank **ascending** on downside beta, so lower sensitivity receives more weight. Leave-one-out market returns reduce the mechanical own-asset overlap in the benchmark. Do not clip negative slopes or combine the score with volatility.

**Variants and selection.** `DOWNBETA63_NEG_a08` uses all observations with leave-one-out benchmark return below zero. `DOWNBETA63_Q25_a08` instead uses observations at or below the 25th percentile of that asset's leave-one-out benchmark returns over the same 63-session window, requiring at least 15 such dates (with 63 observations this may fail; any failure is recorded, not repaired). Select between these within the family using the shared selection rule. This contrasts ordinary down days with the lower tail; no total-beta candidate is added.

**Expected active risk, turnover and cost survival.** The tilt aims for 20.7% active share and roughly 3–8% annualized TE. Expect 15–35% traded notional per 21-session rebalance because downside beta can move with the market regime; the common band and holding all 30 assets are intended to keep it under 50%. The same return-based formula is available for Dow stocks and cross-asset ETFs. It may help risk-adjusted return even if raw return is lower, but 63 observations offer few down-tail samples, so any apparent advantage needs the four test periods and pooled bootstrap to persist.

**Known failure mode.** Low downside beta can be little more than the already tested low total-beta trade, and it can lag in strong bull markets. Conditional slopes estimated from a handful of down days are noisy; a sudden crisis may change exposures faster than the 21-session rebalance. If the left-tail variant lacks 15 qualifying observations, it cannot be silently replaced with an all-day estimate.

**Citation.** Ang, A., Chen, J. and Xing, Y. (2006), “Downside Risk,” *The Review of Financial Studies*, 19(4), 1191–1239. https://doi.org/10.1093/rfs/hhj035.

## Handoff and data limits

No backtests were run for this proposal. The implementer should preregister the common rules above, test exactly the six listed variants on the two selection universes, record per-family frozen choices before opening test results, and report every failed history or data check. OHLCV does not contain futures curves, forward points or ETF holdings needed to measure conventional cross-asset carry; no price-trend measure should be relabeled as carry.
