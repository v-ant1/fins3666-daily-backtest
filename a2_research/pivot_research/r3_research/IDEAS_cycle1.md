# R3 researcher: cycle 1 strategy ideas

## Scope and what prior cycles rule out

This is a proposal for the implementer, not a backtest report. The prior reports show why a signal should be judged on the two registered selection windows and then frozen: the original five-session top-five pivot was costly and failed in later Dow and cross-asset tests; 21-session volatility-scaled reversal had mixed Sharpe results and near-zero excess in cross-assets; inverse-heat allocation did not improve Dow risk-adjusted performance consistently; and the selected 126-session cash momentum gate trailed EW30 in all four tests. Round-one and R2 work also already covers pressure, heat/graph variants, residual and own-return reversal, 63-day total volatility and beta, low-volume reversal, inverse-volatility/HRP/centrality weights, and EW volatility/momentum overlays. The three ideas below use other cross-sectional information in OHLCV. They are hypotheses, not claims that the cited effects will transfer to these small, liquid universes.

The three families have six candidate variants total (two per family), before any EW30 benchmarks. Please disclose every run, including failed or abandoned technical runs. The implementation must use only the Dow 30 and the fixed cross-asset ETF universe; this research did not inspect, download, or test the sealed-holdout universe.

## Common implementation and selection protocol

- **Universe and prices:** The supplied point-in-time Dow roster or fixed 30 cross-asset ETFs. Use adjusted daily OHLCV; when a feature refers to highs, use adjusted highs on the same adjustment basis as adjusted closes. Compute each signal after a close using only completed daily bars through that close. Fill target weights at the next session's open.
- **Portfolio:** Long-only and fully invested across the 30 names, with cash weight zero. For each signal, rank all assets in ascending signal order, use average ranks for ties, and set percentile rank `r_i = (rank_i - 1) / 29` (so the lowest is 0 and highest is 1). Set `w_i = (1/30) * (1 + a * (2*r_i - 1))`. This sums to 100%; at `a=0.8`, target weights range from 0.67% to 6.00%, and at `a=1.0`, from 0% to 6.67%. The formula defines the whole book; do not select a top-five subset.
- **Schedule and band:** Rebalance every 21 eligible sessions, anchored at the registered 2020-11-27 start for the selection run; carry the schedule forward. Compare target weights with the drifted pre-trade weights. If the total absolute target change is below 1.0% of pre-trade NAV, skip that rebalance; otherwise reset all 30 names to target weights. This is the no-trade band. Charge the validated engine's 0.05% per-side cost on actual traded notional, including drift, initial investment and final liquidation; benchmark EW30 uses the same schedule, fills and costs.
- **Selection rule (write/freeze this before producing any results):** Run all six variants on Dow and cross-asset using only 2020-11-27 through 2022-12-30. Exclude a variant if its mean actual traded notional exceeds 50% of pre-trade NAV per rebalance in either universe. Among the remaining variants, select the one with the highest unweighted mean of its annualized daily net Sharpe across the two universes. Break ties by lower two-universe average turnover, then smaller `a`, then the family order listed below. If none qualify, select EW30. Record the winning specification before running any of the four development tests, and run that frozen specification once in each test period. No test result may change the selection. The selection windows are short; this is a mechanical choice rule, not evidence of an expected premium.
- **Variation and trial count:** Six active parameterizations are proposed. Do not add variants or tune thresholds, rebalance dates or bands after seeing results. Add these six active parameterizations to the cumulative trial inventory and note dependence across trials. Earlier reports give somewhat different cumulative estimates (roughly 70–78); the coordinator should use the reconciled count rather than reset it to 76. A no-trade band should be implemented exactly as specified, not calibrated from the results.
- **Reporting:** For the chosen variant, report net return, Sharpe, max drawdown, information ratio and tracking error versus same-universe EW30, actual turnover, block-bootstrap p-value, and deflated Sharpe using the full cumulative trial count. The goal's distinction test is met if annualized tracking error is at least 3% or active share is at least 20%; report both. State plainly if the candidate misses any criterion. A pass on development tests is not a sealed-holdout pass; only the coordinator may run the sealed holdout.

## Idea 1 — Avoid assets with recent lottery-like maximum returns

**Rationale.** A very large positive daily return can make an asset attractive to investors seeking lottery payoffs, potentially depressing its subsequent average return. Bali, Cakici and Whitelaw (2011) study this “MAX” measure and its cross-sectional return relation. This is especially speculative for broad ETFs, where a large daily move may reflect compensation for genuine risk or a structural regime shift rather than lottery demand.

**Exact signal.** For each asset, calculate simple adjusted close-to-close returns for the last 21 completed daily intervals. `MAX21` is the largest of those 21 returns. Rank **ascending** on `-MAX21` (equivalently, rank the smallest MAX21 highest). Do not winsorize, standardize, use intraday highs, or add another signal. Rebalance every 21 sessions under the common band and costs.

**Variants:** `MAX21_a08` with `a=0.8`; `MAX21_a10` with `a=1.0`. The common selection rule chooses between these and the other four variants; it does not pick an idea based on test results.

**Expected active risk and turnover.** By construction, the rank tilt at `a=0.8` has 20% active share under a continuous uniform rank approximation (25% at `a=1.0`); discrete ranks produce nearly the same result. Expect annualized tracking error around 3–8% and approximately 15–40% traded notional per 21-session rebalance before the no-trade band, since the measure is short-window and ranks can change quickly. These are planning ranges, not measured results. Full diversification and slow rebalancing should make turnover and costs far lower than the failed top-five weekly book. Ranking extreme daily close returns is available for both equities and ETFs.

**Known failure mode.** MAX may mainly select commodities, small/high-beta stocks, or assets with real jumps; in a 30-name universe its maximum statistic is noisy, and a short sample can make one event dominate a full month's rank. Flat 5 bp costs also understate spreads and impact in the least-liquid names. It may not be a true lottery-preference effect in the ETF universe.

**Citation.** Bali, T. G., Cakici, N. and Whitelaw, R. F. (2011), “Maxing out: Stocks as lotteries and the cross-section of expected returns,” *Journal of Financial Economics*, 99(2), 427–446. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jfineco.2010.08.014.

## Idea 2 — Proximity to the trailing price high

**Rationale.** A price near its trailing high may reflect information that is incorporated gradually; George and Hwang (2004) show that 52-week-high proximity can explain part of cross-sectional momentum in stocks. This differs from the already tested 6- and 12-month time-series momentum cash gate: here all assets remain invested and are ranked relative to one another by distance from their own trailing high. The cross-asset transfer remains an open question.

**Exact signal.** At a signal close, for each asset compute `proximity_L = adjusted_close[t] / max(adjusted_high[t-L+1 ... t])`, using the current completed session and the previous `L-1` completed sessions. Values are in `(0,1]`. Rank ascending on `proximity_L`, so assets closest to their own trailing high receive higher rank and weight. No cash gate, moving average, return-skip rule, or additional price trend is used. `L=63` and `L=126` have a feasible warmup before the selection start in the supplied cross-asset history beginning 2020-06-01; if any required series does not have the full history on a rebalance date, fail that run rather than shortening its lookback.

**Variants:** `HIGH63_a08` (`L=63`, `a=0.8`); `HIGH126_a08` (`L=126`, `a=0.8`). Apply the shared selection rule across all six variants.

**Expected active risk and turnover.** The fixed rank formula implies about 20% active share at `a=0.8`. The moving-high reference and 21-session schedule should make approximately 10–30% traded notional per rebalance plausible, subject to rank churn and band skips; expected tracking error is about 3–7% annualized. The 0.05% per-side model should therefore be less material than on weekly top-five portfolios. Adjusted highs/closes exist for both universes.

**Known failure mode.** Price near a high may simply load on momentum regimes and crash after reversals; it can lag sharply in mean-reverting markets. A 63/126-session high is also sensitive to one stale extreme, and the original empirical evidence is for individual stocks, not a fixed heterogeneous ETF basket. This family is related to momentum and must be counted separately as another trial, not described as independent evidence.

**Citation.** George, T. J. and Hwang, C.-Y. (2004), “The 52-Week High and Momentum Investing,” *The Journal of Finance*, 59(5), 2145–2176. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-6261.2004.00695.x.

## Idea 3 — Avoid high idiosyncratic volatility, not high total volatility

**Rationale.** Ang, Hodrick, Xing and Zhang (2006) report a negative cross-sectional relation between idiosyncratic volatility and expected stock returns. The earlier project tested each asset's total volatility and beta; this idea instead separates common basket moves from each asset's residual variation. It avoids shorts, leverage and a full factor model. The cited stock result does not establish a premium for ETFs or for this simple one-factor residual estimate.

**Exact signal.** For every asset and each day in the lookback, calculate its adjusted close-to-close simple return `r_i,d`. On the same dates compute `m_d`, the equal-weight average return across the current point-in-time 30-name roster (Dow) or all 30 fixed ETFs (cross-asset). For each asset, fit an OLS regression with intercept `r_i,d = c_i + b_i*m_d + e_i,d` over the trailing `L` completed return observations. The signal is the sample standard deviation of the OLS residuals `e_i,d`, annualized by `sqrt(252)`. Rank ascending (lowest residual volatility gets highest portfolio rank). This is a one-factor idiosyncratic-volatility proxy, not the Fama-French residual measure in Ang et al. If the market proxy has zero variance or history is incomplete, fail the run. Rebalance and cost as in the common protocol.

**Variants:** `IDIO63_a08` (`L=63`, `a=0.8`); `IDIO126_a08` (`L=126`, `a=0.8`). Both lookbacks are available before the first selection rebalance with the supplied cross-asset start; require the complete window. Apply the shared selection rule across all six variants.

**Expected active risk and turnover.** The rank tilt implies about 20% active share at `a=0.8`. The 126-day residual volatility rank should be steadier than the 63-day version; expected turnover is roughly 10–30% of NAV per 21-session rebalance for 126 days and 15–35% for 63 days. A 3–7% annualized tracking error is plausible from the asset concentration, but must be measured. The return and volume histories needed are present in both OHLCV universes; same-constituent ranking and fully invested weights make implementation consistent across them. Costs should remain bounded by monthly-ish rebalancing and all-name holdings.

**Known failure mode.** Residual volatility estimates from only 63–126 observations are noisy and can rank benign asset-specific volatility as harmful risk. A single equal-weight market proxy may not capture sector, duration, currency or commodity factors; results may collapse to the already tested total-volatility effect or become unstable across roster changes. Low idiosyncratic volatility may also not be rewarded in a small 30-asset selection.

**Citation.** Ang, A., Hodrick, R. J., Xing, Y. and Zhang, X. (2006), “The Cross-Section of Volatility and Expected Returns,” *The Journal of Finance*, 61(1), 259–299. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-6261.2006.00836.x.
