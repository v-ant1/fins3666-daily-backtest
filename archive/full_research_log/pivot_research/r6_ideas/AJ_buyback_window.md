# AJ — Post-earnings buyback-window pressure

## Identity and immutable-path preflight

- **Proposed short name and mechanism:** AJ buyback-window pressure. Earnings disclosures reopen some firms’ discretionary issuer-repurchase windows; firms with a larger already-public trailing repurchase amount may therefore create more short-lived post-announcement buying demand.
- **Exact new output path:** `r6_ideas/AJ_buyback_window.md`.
- **Path check:** the exact target did not exist before this screen; this is a new file.
- **Name check:** AJ is a new label in the decision registry; the underlying buyback-pressure mechanism is not new (see §1).
- **Development range / earliest permissible path date:** 2020-11-27 through 2026-10-30; no worker data read before 2020-11-27.
- **Path check:** only development inputs and literature sources were consulted; no holdout path or file was opened. No pre-window filing fields were analyzed; the CSV reader used `filing_date` only to discard rows earlier than the development floor before using any row fields.
- This is a screen only; no signal outcomes, return calculations, or variant are authorized.

## 1. Overlap with prior work

The closest prior screens are `r6_ideas/M_SURVEY.md` (actual issuer repurchase pressure from SEC disclosures), `r6_ideas/W_events.md` §3 (buyback execution windows from 10-Q/K monthly tables), and decision-log items 144 and 196. W already rejected buyback execution timing because the SEC tables aggregate completed purchases by month and do not identify execution days; M found no qualifying positive large-firm continuation evidence. This proposal would use an earnings 8-K as the calendar anchor and a prior, already-public 10-Q/K amount as issuer-intensity weight, rather than claim the table dates the actual purchases.

That timing distinction is a real implementation difference, but it does not make the economics a novel signal: both are issuer buyback pressure, and the 8-K clock merely estimates when a possible pause ends. The mechanism also inherits serious measurement error because blackout rules vary by issuer and prearranged plans can keep buying during a blackout. **Verdict: FAIL — the exact calendarized signal is not identical to the SEC execution-table screen, but it is a close re-expression of a previously rejected buyback family and lacks a demonstrated tradable reopening shock.**

## 2. Exact pressure measure and dated data source

If screened further, define for issuer *i* and earnings event *e*: `intensity_i,e` is the sum of the latest publicly filed four fiscal quarters’ monthly share-repurchase amounts divided by point-in-time market capitalization; the latest filing acceptance must be strictly before the event. `open_i,e` begins on the first full trading session after the Item 2.02 8-K acceptance and lasts 20 trading sessions. The proposed weekly signal would rank names by `intensity_i,e × 1[event window active]`, with the event clock updated only after a qualifying disclosure is public. BMO and intraday filings become usable at that session’s close; an after-close filing first becomes usable at the next session close. The intended prediction horizon is the following five trading sessions, formed at the close after the signal becomes observable. This timing avoids trading at an event close using information released after it.

The earnings clock is derived from the free [SEC EDGAR submissions API](https://www.sec.gov/edgar/sec-api-documentation), using acceptance timestamps and Item 2.02 metadata, joined to point-in-time ticker/CIK and S&P membership records. The SEC filings themselves contain the monthly repurchase table; the table is available only after the filing acceptance, reports past activity rather than future execution, and does not give daily timestamps. Dittmann et al. report repurchase intensity in months with less than 25% estimated blackout days is more than twice that in months with over 75%, in a firm-month panel spanning 2006–2019; this is a documented activity effect, not a return or continuation effect. For this screen I did not fetch or parse any filing tables. A prior input-only audit documents 441 cached 10-Q/K filing metadata rows in the development interval (`r6_ideas/W_events.md`); that is not proof that repurchase tables parse consistently or provide timely amounts.

No outcomes or price data were accessed for this screen. **Verdict: FAIL — the staged clock provides dated earnings filings, but a point-in-time, Dow-mapped repurchase-intensity input is not staged, and 10-Q/K totals cannot verify that issuer buying resumes on the proposed date.**

## 3. Point-in-time coverage and independent breadth

The staged `r6_data/earnings_clock_sp500.csv` was streamed as CSV: each row’s `filing_date` was checked against 2020-11-27 through 2026-10-30 before using its other fields. In that interval the file contains 12,397 Item 2.02 filings (12,333 8-K and 64 8-K/A), 597 ticker labels, 594 distinct CIKs, 1,181 distinct filing dates, 6,622 BMO, 369 intraday, and 5,406 AMC classifications. Earliest/latest included dates are 2020-12-01 and 2026-10-30. These counts are the broad point-in-time S&P earnings-clock coverage, **not** the number of Dow events, valid repurchase tables, or independent post-event return observations; no Dow-specific join was done. The clock QA says filing acceptance is classified from SEC submissions metadata and is not necessarily the time of the scheduled earnings release.

The 30 Dow issuers would yield at most roughly 120 quarterly issuer events per full year before missing filings, non-repurchasers, event overlap, and incomplete 10-Q tables. Many events cluster in earnings seasons and within industries; the nominal issuer-event count therefore overstates independent breadth. Issuer identity can change with ticker changes, merger, and index membership; PIT CIK joins are required. Survivorship is a concern if the Dow roster is not historical. SEC filing acceptance avoids use of the fiscal period end as an information date, but the repurchase table arrives only after the reported purchases. For context, Dittmann, Li, Obernberger and Zheng study monthly US firm observations from 2006–2019 and show repurchase intensity varies with earnings and estimated blackout calendars; the outcome is repurchase activity, not a stock-return premium ([Journal of Accounting and Economics, DOI 10.1016/j.jacceco.2025.101798](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jacceco.2025.101798)).

**Verdict: FAIL — broad event-clock metadata is available, but coverage of the required lagged repurchase amount for the point-in-time Dow cross-section and independent event breadth has not been established.**

## 4. Effective breadth and power at a sourced plausible effect

There is no published effect estimate matching the proposed post-earnings reopening signal for large-cap individual stocks. Cziraki, Lyandres and Michaely (2021) is often cited in this context, but its result is that insider net buying increases before open-market repurchase **announcements** and predicts announcement/long-run outcomes; it does not estimate issuer repurchase execution restarting after an earnings 8-K ([Journal of Corporate Finance 66, 101544, DOI 10.1016/j.jcorpfin.2019.101544](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jcorpfin.2019.101544)). Dittmann, Li, Obernberger and Zheng study monthly US firm observations from 2006–2019 and show repurchase intensity varies with earnings and estimated blackout calendars; the firm-month outcome is repurchase activity (with firm-clustered standard errors), not a stock-return premium ([Journal of Accounting and Economics 80(1), 101798, DOI 10.1016/j.jacceco.2025.101798](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jacceco.2025.101798)). A State Street Global Advisors study uses the S&P 500 Buyback Index’s top 100 repurchase-ratio firms and 136 monthly chain-linked periods from 1994–2018; it reports no significant differences in the measured prior, earnings, or post-earnings periods, including no significant post-earnings factor alpha ([SSGA, 2019](https://www.ssga.com/library-content/pdfs/etf/us/b27-buyback-blackout-periods-do-not-negatively-impact-performance.pdf)). This is evidence against a useful blackout-return effect, not a precise zero estimate for this proposed issuer-level rank IC.

For a transparent sensitivity only, `r4_harness/power_sim.py` was run with 30 assets, 1,500 dates, 5-session horizon/NW lag 4, AR(1) signal persistence 0.5, event rate 0.5 per trading date (about 120 events/year across 30 issuers), negative-binomial cluster shape 0.5, 10,000 simulations, seed 20261111. At a **hypothetical, unsourced** event-date mean rank IC of 0.05, simulated power for NW *t* ≥ 2 is 97.99%; mean nonzero dates 439.2, effective dates 197.4, and 80%-power MDE 0.0287. The event count and correlation assumptions are planning assumptions; the harness does not model industry clusters or uncertainty in repurchase-table availability. Since the literature supplies no positive large-cap post-event IC to compare with the 0.0287 MDE, the sensitivity result is not evidence of feasibility. **Verdict: FAIL — no matched, credible positive effect size exists; 0.05 was only a power illustration.**

## 5. Criterion-4 portfolio feasibility first

The contemplated book is a weekly, long-only, rank-weighted top-half tilt among the eligible 30-name Dow, compared with EW30; each rebalance must retain at least 50% active share and total turnover must average no more than 50% per rebalance. Execution cost is 0.05% per side; the six-year decision would require positive net excess, Sharpe above EW30, one-sided block-bootstrap evidence after Holm correction, and must retain the goal’s criterion-4 thresholds. The current R6 Holm family manifest has 21 endpoints and will grow as new endpoints are registered. The 2026 bound-by-shape audit puts the weekly RankTopHalf 80%-power threshold near 0.074 rank IC at low persistence (`ρ=0.2`) and 0.093 at `ρ=0.8`; those are synthetic planning bounds, not observed effects. At a monthly cadence the analogous threshold is 0.120–0.247. The required effect is thus large relative to the absence of any documented post-window mega-cap return premium.

No defensible positive gross excess Sharpe can be derived from the cited evidence. Treating the literature’s lack of significant post-event alpha as a conservative gross edge of zero, then applying the turnover cap at weekly cadence, gives at least 0.05% × 0.50 × 52 = **1.30% annual trading-cost drag** if turnover is measured as total traded notional per rebalance (or 2.60% if 50% is one-way per side). Under the bound audit’s RankTopHalf active variance factor 0.0379 and 6% monthly cross-sectional dispersion, active annualized volatility is approximately 4.1%; the zero-gross case therefore implies an incremental net Sharpe of about **−0.32** using the 1.30% cost floor (about −0.63 under the one-way turnover interpretation). This arithmetic is a cost-floor sensitivity, not a forecast; an event score could churn more. A bootstrap *p* is not estimable without portfolio return paths; with a current 21-endpoint Holm family, the first-step cutoff is 0.05/21 ≈ 0.00238 and the family may grow. The portfolio cannot plausibly clear the 0.074 weekly rank-IC hurdle on current evidence. **Verdict: FAIL — criterion-4 feasibility is not established, and costs make the zero-effect case strictly worse than EW30.**

## Final screen decision

- **Overall: STOP — FAIL.** This is a close relative of the already rejected issuer-buyback family, the proposed reopening indicator does not observe actual execution, no matched post-2020 mega-cap effect supports power or criterion 4, and the required lagged repurchase-intensity panel is not staged or coverage-audited.
- Evidence that could change the decision: a genuinely new and free point-in-time issuer buyback execution dataset with dates and amounts for the historical Dow, plus independent post-2010 evidence showing a sufficiently large post-earnings return effect after costs. Re-estimating or relabeling the existing 10-Q/K aggregate proxy would not change the decision.
