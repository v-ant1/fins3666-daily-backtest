## Common feasibility and power setup

The development date window is **2020-11-27 through 2026-10-30 only**. Using the already-staged `r6_data/sp500_ohlcv_dev.parquet`, I read only its `date` and `ticker` columns after filtering to that window: 716,987 rows cover all **1,488** NYSE sessions in the interval, with 461–503 represented tickers on event dates (median 483). These counts show that a broad PIT S&P cross-section can be assembled, not that event pressure is measurable or predictive. The underlying QA separately records that the S&P OHLCV panel is incomplete for 51 PIT member tickers; any later mechanism test would need a fixed missing-data rule. The Dow development panel and its 2020-11-27 opening coverage are already staged, but a 30-stock sample does not solve the lack of independent calendar events.

For each candidate, the power check models one cross-sectional rank IC per event date, with **500 nominal S&P names**, **54 effective SIC2 clusters**, assumed within-SIC2 IC correlation 0.15, and therefore assumed IC standard deviation `sqrt([1 + (500/54 − 1) × 0.15] / 53) = 0.20538`. The ICC is an explicit sensitivity assumption, not estimated from development outcomes. I ran `r4_harness/power_sim.py` for 20,000 synthetic draws, planted IC 0.02, AR(1) 0.20, five-session outcome/dependence horizon (NW lag 4), with the same event counts as the development calendar. The planted 0.02 is only a common planning benchmark; no paper supplies a convertible Dow rank-IC for these exact designs.

| Direction | Event dates | Effective N (mean) | P(NW t ≥ 2) at planted IC 0.02 | MC SE | 80% MDE |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Turn of month | 72 | 52.36 | 13.83% | 0.00244 | 0.08065 |
| Pre-holiday | 58 | 42.18 | 12.71% | 0.00235 | 0.08986 |
| Quarter-end | 24 | 17.45 | 12.98% | 0.00238 | 0.13969 |

This is synthetic power only, not evidence about an actual signal. Even the S&P-wide idealized rank test would need a very large mean IC to attain 80% power; using the Dow or additionally clustering standard errors by event would make the portfolio decision less feasible. A one-event long-only round trip costs at least 10 bp at the project's 5 bp per-side assumption before impact, so an actual event-time position would need a substantial edge after costs.

## 1. Turn-of-month institutional liquidity / payment-cycle flow

**Candidate definition for screening.** A plausible cross-sectional proxy would rank each stock by its average close-location-volume pressure over the last five sessions before the month-end settlement-liquidity window, standardized to its own trailing non-event sessions, and test the first five sessions of the next month. This is not the Etula signal: the paper's result concerns institutional cash needs and broad market/liquidity effects. The proposed daily OHLCV proxy has no signed trades or observed payroll/fund cash flows. “Payday” is therefore not an independently observed stock-level variable here.

### Gates (a)–(e)

- **(a) Literature — PASS for the institutional calendar mechanism.** Etula, Rinne, Suominen, and Vaittinen, [“Dash for Cash: Monthly Market Impact of Institutional Liquidity Needs,” *Review of Financial Studies* 33 (2020), 75–111](https://doi.org/10.1093/rfs/hhz054), document monthly payment-cycle patterns in liquid markets and temporary changes in debt/equity capital costs around month-end cash-need dates; their evidence attributes part of the pattern to institutions. Ogden, [“Turn-of-Month Evaluations of Liquid Profits and Stock Returns,” *Journal of Finance* 45 (1990), 1259–1272](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-6261.1990.tb02435.x), relates the U.S. payment schedule to monthly stock-index returns, but the paper's index-level result does not give a cross-sectional company-pressure effect. These papers document a calendar-wide mechanism, not the proposed stock-level pressure score.
- **(b) Mega-cap / post-2020 case — PARTIAL historically, FAIL for this proposed Dow cross-section.** Etula et al. report larger effects for larger, more liquid stocks and stocks more commonly held by mutual funds, which is unusually relevant to the Dow. But there is no post-2020 Dow estimate in the cited evidence, no daily observed fund flows in the free staged inputs, and a common month-end flow shock cannot by itself explain which Dow name should outperform another. The cash-settlement calendar itself changed from T+2 to T+1 during development, so a preregistration would also need an exact, historically correct event clock.
- **(c) Power — FAIL.** The common 72-event power check above yields 13.83% detection at IC 0.02 and an 80% MDE of 0.08065. The literature does not give an effect size in compatible cross-sectional rank-IC units to support that large an effect. C2 already used the month-end liquidity-pressure mechanism and failed the prior idea gate on event breadth and stock-level network specificity; the current proposal does not fix those limitations.
- **(d) Free-data feasibility — PARTIAL.** Event dates and daily OHLCV exist for the development interval, and a simple date/ticker coverage check passed. Payroll calendars are not enough to measure paycheck inflows; fund transaction or daily institutional cash-flow data are not part of the staged free inputs. OHLCV does not establish order sign, while `close location × volume` would reduce the signal to a calendar interaction on the original candle-pressure ingredients. At 5 bp per side, one full entry/exit event costs at least 10 bp before spread/impact.
- **(e) Novelty / overlap — FAIL.** `r6_ideas/C.md` already screened the same Etula month-end cash-demand idea as C2 and ruled it out for no unique cross-stock transmission and too few independent events. `r6_ideas/I.md` separately tests recurring calendar-month returns; R1 tests predicted dividend-paying months. Replacing the month dummy with OHLCV measured in that same window is not sufficiently distinct to reopen the rejected direction, and it cannot be used as a rescue.

**Disposition: KILL as duplicate and underpowered.**

## 2. Pre-holiday pressure / reduced selling

**Candidate definition for screening.** A possible cross-sectional version would compare each name's pre-holiday close-location-volume score with its own normal sessions and rank names on that abnormal score for a short post-holiday window. This shifts the original market-level return premium into a stock-selection problem that the cited studies do not establish. A calendar-only long Dow position would be a timing overlay, not a cross-sectional buying-pressure signal, and is outside this candidate's requested form.

### Gates (a)–(e)

- **(a) Literature — PASS for historical aggregate pre-holiday returns; FAIL for the required stock-level claim.** Ariel, [“High Stock Returns before Holidays,” *Journal of Finance* 45 (1990), 1611–1626](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-6261.1990.tb03731.x), reports that the pre-holiday trading day had unusually high average returns in 1963–1982, with a post-test extension through 1986. Keim, [“Trading Patterns, Bid-Ask Spreads, and Estimated Security Returns,” *Journal of Financial Economics* 25 (1989), 75–97](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2014/03/Keim-JFE1989.pdf), shows that apparent seasonal price patterns can partly reflect bid-ask effects. Neither establishes an exploitable Dow cross-sectional signal after the holiday.
- **(b) Mega-cap / post-2020 case — FAIL.** Ko and Yang, [“The Pre-Holiday Premium of Ariel (1990) Has Largely Become a Small-Firm Effect Out of Sample,” *Critical Finance Review* 13 (2024), 531–538](https://doi.org/10.1561/104.00000111), extend the test through 2019 and report that the premium now exists only among small firms. The old index-level result therefore provides poor support for liquid mega-caps, and no cited evidence shows the effect persisting among Dow stocks after 2020. A common-market premium would cancel substantially in Dow relative-return ranks.
- **(c) Power — FAIL.** The 58 pre-holiday dates in the observed development calendar give only 12.71% power at planted IC 0.02 and 80% MDE 0.08986 under the same SIC2-cluster assumptions. This is already optimistic because the dates are common market events, not independent firm-level shocks; a single common event could invalidate a cross-sectional date-level test. The published aggregate pre-holiday means cannot validly be converted to a stock-level rank IC.
- **(d) Free-data feasibility — PARTIAL for the calendar, FAIL for direct pressure.** The date/ticker-only pull found all 58 calendar event sessions in the S&P panel, with median 484 names. Daily OHLCV offers intraday range and close location but not signed trades or pre-holiday buyer identity. A trailing firm-specific event history begins only in the development sample and provides at most a few dozen observations, too little to estimate stable stock-level exposure. The holiday return itself is observable only at that session's close; a close-formed signal cannot trade the same-session premium. A next-session signal has no directly documented positive return effect.
- **(e) Novelty / overlap — PARTIAL but insufficient.** This is not the same as R1's predicted dividend schedule or I's same-calendar-month return score, but the evidence is another broad calendar anomaly rather than a new measurable cross-sectional pressure network. C2 has already rejected the related month-end calendar-flow translation. No free source in scope supplies a new holiday-specific institution or investor-flow measure, so this remains a re-timing of OHLCV rather than a clearly novel pressure mechanism.

**Disposition: KILL; the survival evidence runs against mega-cap continuation.**

## 3. Quarter-end institutional positioning / window dressing

**Candidate definition for screening.** At each quarter-end, measure a stock's abnormal close-location-volume pressure in the final five sessions relative to its own trailing 60 non-quarter-end sessions. Pre-register one sign rather than selecting after results; a conservative pressure-reversal formulation would predict that the strongest positive temporary quarter-end pressure reverses over the first five sessions of the new quarter. The signal is an OHLCV proxy for institutionally timed trading, not a direct holdings or execution measure.

### Gates (a)–(e)

- **(a) Literature — PASS for quarter-end institutional trading changes, NOT for the specified return signal.** He, Ng, and Wang, [“Quarterly Trading Patterns of Financial Institutions,” *Journal of Business* 77 (2004), 493–509](https://doi.org/10.1086/386527), report that institutions sell poorly performing stocks more in Q4 than other quarters, with stronger behavior among institutions whose portfolios have underperformed; some self-managed institutions show less window dressing. Gormley, Kaplan, and Verma, [“More Informative Disclosures, Less Informative Prices? Portfolio and Price Formation around Quarter-Ends,” *Journal of Financial Economics* 146 (2022), 665–688](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jfineco.2021.10.004), use institutional transaction data and find funds complete existing position-building at quarter-end but delay initiating new campaigns until the next quarter; quarter-end returns are reported to be 32% less informative about 30-day changes than returns on other days. This supports changed trade timing and price formation, not a general positive/negative stock-level return drift from daily candles.
- **(b) Mega-cap / post-2020 case — PARTIAL mechanism, FAIL effect transport.** Gormley et al. provide relatively recent evidence that disclosure-related trading schedules can affect prices. However, their Ancerno institutional transaction data are not free staged project inputs, and their result does not say which large stock will experience buying versus selling. He et al.'s institutional behavior is heterogeneous and portfolio-performance dependent, which means “quarter-end” alone does not fix the sign. No direct post-2020 Dow mega-cap effect size is documented.
- **(c) Power — FAIL.** The 24 quarter-end event dates imply 12.98% power for a planted 0.02 mean IC and an 80% MDE of 0.13969 using SIC2-clustered S&P assumptions. That MDE is extreme and the quarter-end dates are the limiting units; 500 stocks do not create 500 independent quarter-end shocks. The literature's 32% decline in price informativeness is not an expected-return effect, much less a rank-IC, and cannot justify planting a larger mean.
- **(d) Free-data feasibility — PARTIAL.** All 24 quarter-end sessions are present in the development date/ticker panel, with 461–501 names per session (median 483). OHLCV can support a fixed close-location/volume proxy, but it cannot distinguish institutional from retail trades, buy from sell order flow, or portfolio window dressing from routine institutional execution. A 10-Q/13F filings calendar and quarterly holdings snapshot would not recover the daily trade flow, and holdings become public with a lag. Even with a monthly/quarterly rebalance, each complete entry and exit costs 10 bp at the assumed 5 bp per side, before market impact.
- **(e) Novelty / overlap — PARTIAL, not enough.** This differs from R1's dividend schedule and I's recurring monthly return signal: the proposed observable is quarter-end-conditioned daily pressure. But it is very close to earlier window-dressing, fund-flow, and event-pressure searches in the R6 idea portfolio; its only new ingredient is the calendar filter on the same OHLCV pressure already used elsewhere. Unless a free direct daily institutional-flow measure is found before any outcome testing, this is not a sufficiently distinct new mechanism to warrant a variant.

**Disposition: KILL pending a data source not currently staged and a larger independent event history.**

## Overall gate decision

No new seasonal-flow direction is ready to advance. Turn-of-month pressure has the best literature support for liquid large stocks but is a previously rejected C2 idea and overlaps the broad seasonal work in I; quarter-end has newer institutional trade-timing evidence but no signed, free stock-level flow signal and only 24 events; the pre-holiday effect is now reported primarily for small firms and does not imply a Dow cross-section. The existing S&P date/ticker panel is sufficient for an input availability check, but coverage does not supply the missing direct flow variable or overcome the low event-count power. **Verdict: nothing credible remains in this calendar-flow family under the current gates.**
