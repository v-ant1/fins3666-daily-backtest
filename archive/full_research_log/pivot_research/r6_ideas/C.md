The probabilities below are subjective chances of clearing the *entire* R6 stack, including development prediction, placebo, net portfolio, multiple-testing and eventual holdout criteria.

## Shared cost convention for a top-5 weekly design

For an apples-to-apples scale check, assume a 30-stock cross-section, 4% weekly single-stock return standard deviation, and a normal-score top-5 selection coefficient of about 1.4 versus the equal-weight universe. These are explicit planning assumptions, not measured project inputs. A Spearman IC of 0.0183 corresponds approximately to a Pearson correlation of 0.0192 near zero, giving an illustrative gross top-5 active return of `0.0192 × 4% × 1.4 = 0.108%`, or **10.8 bp per week**.

At 0.05% per side, a 50% one-way turnover cap (half the portfolio traded in and half out, 100% total buy-plus-sell notional) costs **5 bp per weekly rebalance**, or about **2.6% per year** over 52 weeks. The illustrative edge then leaves 5.8 bp/week, about 1.5% simple annualized. If names are fully replaced every week, buy-plus-sell notional is 200% and cost is **10 bp/week** (5.2% annualized), almost all of the illustrative edge. Spread, market impact and borrow would worsen these numbers. Thus the 0.0183 IC hurdle is only potentially tradable if turnover is held near or below 50%; it is not a free pass on portfolio costs. Each direction must show its own measured turnover before portfolio work.

## 1. Overnight/intraday clientele pressure spreading to industry peers

**Gate: FAIL-GATE as written; highest-ranked candidate for a small, frozen mechanism check.**

**(a) Literature verified.** Lou, Polk and Skouras, [“A tug of war: Overnight versus intraday expected returns,” *Journal of Financial Economics* 134 (2019), 192–213](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jfineco.2019.03.011), studies US equities over 1993–2013. The authors find persistent overnight and intraday components, interpret the split as pressure from distinct investor clienteles, and report that the large-cap value-weight momentum portfolio earned 1.24% monthly (t=2.17), with 0.80% overnight (t=2.50) and 0.44% intraday (t=1.24). Their large-cap results therefore include post-2010 observations, but the paper does not publish a separate 2011–13-only estimate or establish persistence through 2020–26. Heston, Korajczyk and Sadka, [“Intraday Patterns in the Cross-section of Stock Returns,” *Journal of Finance* 65 (2010), 1369–1407](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-6261.2010.01573.x), find half-hour return continuation at matching times of day that lasts at least 40 trading days, consistent with recurring flows; this is a different intraday-data effect, not evidence for the proposed daily peer graph.

**(b) Large-cap case.** The clientele explanation is credible in liquid firms because institutions are important there, and Lou et al. explicitly report stronger overnight/intraday patterns among large stocks. Their data reach 2013, so they establish some post-2010 evidence, but do not prove a current tradable edge. More arbitrage capital, ETF execution and faster information incorporation could have weakened it since then. The proposed peer extension would test source pressure against a free, ex-ante industry graph (same 4-digit SIC from SEC company records); it must show incremental prediction beyond each stock’s own overnight, intraday and close-to-close return. Same-industry labels can create dense groups and do not establish causal links, so shuffled links and within-industry placebo links are essential.

**(c) Cluster-aware power and cost.** Development dates 2020-11-27 through 2026-10-30 provide roughly 307 weekly observations. With weekly IC autocorrelation assumed AR(1)=0.40, the time-series effective count is `307 × (1−0.40)/(1+0.40) ≈ 132 weeks`. For a Dow-only weekly rank-IC series with null SD 0.20, 80% power at two-sided 5% requires about `(1.96+0.84)×0.20/√132 = 0.049` IC, well above 0.0183. A stacked stock-week regression is less pessimistic but still marginal: assume 30 stocks and within-week residual correlation ρ=0.15, giving cross-sectional design effect `1+29×0.15=5.35`; joint effective N is `132×30/5.35≈740`, and with residual SD 0.20 the 80%-power detectable effect is about `2.8×0.20/√740=0.021`. This is only a stylized cluster calculation; dependence induced by industry links may be stronger. It says Dow-only evidence at 0.0183 is not safely powered. A 500-name S&P sample can improve the mechanism test, but all names still share dates and graph clusters; its effective N and MDE must be simulated with date and industry-block resampling, not counted as 500 independent daily observations. At the common cost convention above, 0.0183 IC leaves little cushion at 50% turnover and essentially none at full replacement. **Gate condition:** stop if the ex-ante cluster simulation, using actual available roster and graph sizes but no outcomes, cannot show 80% power at IC 0.0183–0.026.

**(d) Data feasibility.** Daily Dow OHLCV is already in the project. It allows `overnight = open_t/close_(t−1)−1` and `intraday = close_t/open_t−1` without paid TAQ, though daily OHLC is not signed order flow and the open may be a noisy execution price. SEC company identity/SIC records are free. The S&P point-in-time roster file `r6_data/sp500_pit_roster.csv` exists, but this review checked file presence only, not coverage, correctness or any outcome columns. First pull should be limited to row counts, ticker coverage and classification stability for the development dates. A full daily intraday-bars dataset is unnecessary for the initial hypothesis, but the paper’s half-hour mechanism cannot be replicated from OHLCV.

**(e) Novelty.** The overnight/intraday split and its persistence are established. The new question is whether a source stock’s overnight-versus-intraday pressure predicts subsequent returns among pre-linked industry peers, over and above own-stock persistence and market/industry returns. This is a genuine network extension, but only if graph links are frozen from external industry classifications and a shuffled-graph placebo fails; otherwise it collapses to a standard overnight/intraday return sort. No parameter sweep: preregister one pressure definition, one peer-weight rule and one primary horizon, at most three variants.

## 2. Month-end cash-demand pressure across rates and linked equities

**Gate: FAIL-GATE.**

**(a) Literature verified.** Etula, Rinne, Suominen and Vaittinen, [“Dash for Cash: Monthly Market Impact of Institutional Liquidity Needs,” *Review of Financial Studies* 33 (2020), 75–111](https://doi.org/10.1093/rfs/hhz054), document temporary increases in debt and equity capital costs around dates associated with month-end cash needs; the working-paper description attributes selling to pension funds ahead of month-end liquidity needs. Lakonishok and Smidt, [“Are Seasonal Anomalies Real? A Ninety-Year Perspective,” *Journal of Business* 61 (1988), 403–425](https://doi.org/10.1086/296445), examine long-run Dow returns and report recurring calendar patterns including turn-of-month behavior. The second paper supports the existence of calendar concentration in a large-cap index, but not the cash-demand mechanism or an investable daily cross-asset signal.

**(b) Large-cap case.** Institutional cash calls and settlement dates apply to large liquid markets as well as small stocks; a temporary pressure effect could remain when flows are synchronized. Yet the cited “Dash for Cash” result is a market-level monthly phenomenon and does not establish that one company’s pressure spreads to its linked peers. Proposed network channel: cash-demand stress in Treasury instruments followed by reversal in Dow stocks with high ex-ante rate/financing sensitivity, using a fixed public industry/sector graph. This is a weaker relation than a direct supply-chain or common-holder edge, and the paper’s published evidence does not justify the specific equity ranking. Synchronized month-end trading can also be arbitraged by predictable rebalancing.

**(c) Cluster-aware power and cost.** At most 72 month-end clusters exist in the six-year development window. If monthly event returns have AR(1)=0.30, `N_eff≈72×0.70/1.30≈39`. With daily event-return SD assumed 1.5%, the 80%-power detectable mean per event is `2.8×1.5%/√39≈0.67%`; this is much larger than a plausible few-basis-point equity calendar premium. Multiple names do not multiply independent event dates; if tested as a cross-section, use two-way date/sector clustering. A tailored event trade that enters and exits once per month has 100% one-way turnover per event and 200% buy-plus-sell notional: **10 bp per cycle** at 5 bp/side, or 1.2% per year for 12 cycles, before implementation slippage. A weekly top-5 translation instead inherits the shared 5–10 bp weekly cost case above and is unlikely to be justified by a small calendar effect. The event-count math fails the power gate before portfolio testing.

**(d) Data feasibility.** Dow OHLCV and SEC industry labels are free. Treasury auction and settlement calendars are public, and FRED offers public rate series, but a precise historical *institutional cash need / flow* measure is not the same as an auction calendar or Treasury yield. A no-cost feasibility pull can establish date coverage of auction/settlement schedules and daily rates without touching equity outcomes. If the hypothesis requires proprietary fund transactions or flow data, stop. No broad or point-in-time S&P roster outcome work is needed for this check.

**(e) Novelty.** Testing whether Treasury cash-demand pressure temporally leads a sector-linked equity response differs from the original market-level month-end finding. It is not novel if reduced to buying Dow stocks around the turn of the month. The network and cross-asset link must be pre-specified and add information beyond a calendar dummy; otherwise this is a well-known seasonal effect with too few independent dates. Given the power limit, do not use an R6 strategy slot without a much larger genuinely independent history, which is outside the authorized development data.

## 3. Deep-in-the-money call-exercise selling pressure at expiration

**Gate: FAIL-GATE.**

**(a) Literature verified.** Roll, Schwartz and Subrahmanyam, [“Stock Price Clustering on Option Expiration Dates,” *Journal of Financial Economics* 78 (2005), 331–361](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jfineco.2004.08.005), find optionable stocks clustered more around strike prices on expiration dates in 1996–2002, while emphasizing the limited evidence of broad underlying-stock price impact. Chen and Lu, [“Stock returns on option expiration dates: Price impact of liquidity trading,” *Journal of Empirical Finance* 28 (2014), 273–290](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jempfin.2014.03.003), report that stocks with sufficiently large deeply in-the-money call open interest relative to underlying trading volume fall about 0.8 percentage point on expiration dates, followed by a short-term reversal; they attribute it to exercised-call holders selling the acquired shares. This is a narrow exposed-stock result, not general expiration-Friday pressure. Neither cited paper establishes persistence for mega-cap stocks after 2010.

**(b) Large-cap case.** Large-cap options are liquid and their underlying shares are easy to trade, but their deep equity liquidity also absorbs exercise-related selling. The 2014 paper’s 0.8% finding is conditional on substantial exercised call OI relative to stock volume. Its sample/effect is pre- or around 2010, not proof of the mechanism surviving the post-2010 expansion of weekly and same-day options. No credible post-2010 large-cap survival case is established here. A network extension to peers is especially speculative: a contract’s exercise flow is issuer-specific unless an independently defined common-options or industry link transmits the pressure.

**(c) Cluster-aware power and cost.** There are roughly 72 standard monthly expiration Fridays in six development years; weekly expirations are not independent clusters when the same underlying names and market conditions recur. With AR(1)=0.30, `N_eff≈39`. At 1.5% daily residual volatility, the 80%-power detectable event response is about `2.8×1.5%/√39=0.67%`. The published 0.8% effect would barely clear this stylized threshold **only if** it transferred unchanged to the target stocks and the exposed-call subset were known without measurement error; neither assumption is credible enough for a power pass. On an event-only long/short trade with full 100% one-way entry and exit per monthly expiry, costs are 10 bp/event (1.2% annualized for 12 events). The paper’s reported 80 bp is gross and conditional, but the signal is unavailable free, and selecting the exposed small subset would raise turnover and data error. Weekly top-5 selection would again need roughly 10.8 bp gross per week at IC 0.0183 just to beat 10 bp full-replacement costs; there is no evidence the expiration signal has that broad weekly edge.

**(d) Data feasibility.** Dow OHLCV is free but cannot identify deeply in-the-money call OI, exercise exposure, or call-minus-put positions. Cboe’s official Open-Close dataset describes historical option volume/open-interest fields and a paid subscription; its own documentation notes that open-interest conventions shifted by one day in portions of its history. This is not a free historical chain suitable for a clean 2020–26 test. No paid purchase or UNSW license was verified in this gate. Without a free, point-in-time option OI source, the signal cannot be rebuilt without look-ahead or proxy substitution; reject before using an R6 variant.

**(e) Novelty.** The issuer-level expiration pressure/reversal mechanism is already directly studied by Chen and Lu. The only possible contribution is a post-2010 mega-cap replication plus an external-link spillover test, but there is no available free option input and no established peer mechanism. A calendar-only expiration strategy would not be a novel pressure measure. Reconsider only if a free, validated historical option OI feed appears and a literature check finds post-2010 large-cap evidence; otherwise this is a predictable data-feasibility failure.

## Final recommendation

Do not promote any of the three directly to an outcome test. If one idea is kept in the backlog, use only direction 1 and first run a no-outcome roster/classification/price-coverage check plus a synthetic date-and-industry-block power simulation. Proceed to a frozen preregistration only if that simulation reaches the R6 power target around IC 0.0183–0.026 and the network adds an identifiable component beyond own overnight/intraday pressure. The monthly and options ideas fail now on independent-cluster power and/or required free data, respectively.
