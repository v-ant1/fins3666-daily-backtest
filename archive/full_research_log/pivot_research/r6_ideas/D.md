# R6 Idea Gate — D: FINRA flow pressure

**Scope.** This is a proposal and data-feasibility review only. I downloaded recent FINRA flow samples and checked ticker/date coverage and row counts; I did not calculate prices, returns, signals, predictive results, or portfolio outcomes. The only pre-holdout-era check was one 2010 Reg SHO file's existence and row count; I did not inspect its fields or symbols. No sealed-holdout price or return data were accessed.

## Cheap pull completed first

- **Reg SHO daily short-sale volume:** 43 raw consolidated-NMS daily files for 2026-09-01 through 2026-10-30, from FINRA's public file endpoint. They contain 12,216–12,466 data rows per date and all 30 securities in the project's pinned 2024-11-08 Dow roster on every sampled date. September has 21 files and October has 22. Row counts and ticker coverage only were recorded; the short-volume values were not analysed. Raw files and row-count manifests are in [`r6_data/finra_sample`](../r6_data/finra_sample/README.md).
- **OTC non-ATS weekly transparency:** two raw FINRA API responses for week starts 2026-08-24 and 2026-08-31, filtered to the same 30 project Dow names. The files contain 459 and 461 symbol-firm records and all 30 names in each week. This is firm/security aggregate weekly volume and trade counts, not a buy/sell imbalance. See the raw JSON and manifest in the same folder.
- **Historical availability:** `https://regsho.finra.org/CNMSshvol20100104.txt` was available and had **738 data rows** (header/trailer excluded); this was a row-count-only check. FINRA's OTC transparency documentation says weekly reporting began on 2016-04-25, so OTC non-ATS weekly data cannot extend to 2010. FINRA's API describes its historical weekly summary as a rolling four-year archive; this review did not claim a 2010 or pre-2020 weekly pull. The sample and history limits are documented in the manifest/README.

The official sources describe Reg SHO daily short volume as trades reported to FINRA facilities, and FINRA cautions that it is not short interest and excludes activity not publicly disseminated or reported off-exchange. The daily file can include a short sale that is covered on the same day, including short sales used to facilitate another customer's buy order. Likewise, the OTC weekly file reports aggregate share quantity/trade count by security and firm; it does not label buyer/seller side or retail identity. These differences determine which hypotheses the data can actually test. [FINRA daily data guide](https://www.finra.org/finra-data/browse-catalog/short-sale-volume-data/daily-short-sale-volume-files), [FINRA short-interest explanation](https://www.finra.org/investors/insights/short-interest), [FINRA OTC weekly API fields and history](https://www.finra.org/sites/default/files/OTC-Transparency-Data-File-Download-API-v04.pdf).

## Ranking and gate decisions

|---:|---|---|---:|---|
| 1 | Persistent FINRA short-sale flow in the same stock | **FAIL-GATE as written** | 5% | The strongest evidence and a free daily data path, but the regulator's volume measure is a noisy proxy for bearish pressure and the conservative Dow power case misses the IC hurdle. |
| 2 | Short-sale pressure from a source company spreading to its supplier/customer peers | **FAIL-GATE** | 2% | It adds a real external link, but event-cluster power is poor and the free network has not been coverage-checked. |

Probabilities are reviewer judgments for the entire R6 process, including a frozen mechanism result, a working placebo, net returns after costs, multiple-testing correction, turnover rule and the one-shot holdout. They are not estimated from project results.

## Shared turnover and cost calculation

The project harness defines total turnover as all traded notional (buys plus sells) divided by NAV, with cost equal to `0.0005 × turnover` per rebalance. For a top-5-of-30 weekly portfolio, take a planning cross-sectional weekly return dispersion of 4% and the harness's top-fraction coefficient, `sqrt(12) × (0.5 − 1/12) = 1.587`. The illustrative gross top-5 active return is therefore `weekly dispersion × coefficient × rank IC`:

- IC **0.0183**: `4% × 1.587 × 0.0183 = 0.116%`, or **11.6 bp/week** gross.
- IC **0.0260**: `4% × 1.587 × 0.0260 = 0.165%`, or **16.5 bp/week** gross.
- **50% total-notional turnover** costs 2.5 bp/week (0.05%/side × 0.50); it consumes 21.5% of the 11.6 bp low-hurdle gross edge and satisfies the R6 turnover limit.
- A full weekly replacement of five holdings trades about 200% of NAV (sell 100%, buy 100%): cost is **10 bp/week**, or about **5.2%/year**. That consumes 86% of the 11.6 bp edge and 61% of the 16.5 bp edge, and fails the R6 turnover/cost condition unless the portfolio can actually stay inside the 50% cap.

These are planning conversions, not results. They omit spread, impact and any borrow fee; an event long/short design also pays borrow. The signal is not investable unless its *measured* turnover is capped at 50% per rebalance or its costs are shown to be under one quarter of gross edge.

## 1. Persistent same-stock Reg SHO short-sale flow

**Gate: FAIL-GATE as written; highest-ranked candidate for one preregistered mechanism test if power is accepted.**

**(a) Verified evidence.** Diether, Lee and Werner, [“Short-Sale Strategies and Return Predictability,” *Review of Financial Studies* 22 (2009), 575–607](https://doi.org/10.1093/rfs/hhn047), use the SEC's 2005 short-sale data and find short sellers increase trading after positive returns and predict negative abnormal returns; their paper explicitly reports that daily-activity trading costs could wipe out profits. The data and disclosure regime differ from today's FINRA files. Wang, Yan and Zheng, [“Shorting Flows, Public Disclosure, and Market Efficiency,” *Journal of Financial Economics* 135 (2020), 191–212](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jfineco.2019.05.018), study publicly disclosed daily short-sale flows in 2010–2015. They report that persistent, long-term shorting predicts negative returns (including a value-weighted result), but abnormal short-term flow increases do **not** predict future returns or anticipate bad news. This is useful post-2010 large-cap evidence, but it argues against treating a one-day spike as a clean pressure shock.

**(b) Mega-cap survival.** Large institutions and market makers trade Dow names, so an accumulated short-sale flow can reflect information or inventory pressure even in deep markets. Wang et al.'s value-weighted result makes a large-cap continuation test more credible than a small-stock-only anomaly. Yet their signal is accumulated short flow, not FINRA's daily short-volume ratio as a stand-alone forecast, and public disclosure creates an obvious arbitrage channel. FINRA warns that short volume can include same-day covering and liquidity-facilitating short sales; it is not an open short position and does not cover all markets. The result may persist as information but need not be temporary selling pressure. No evidence establishes that the edge survived past 2015, much less in the current daily data.

**(c) Power and cost.** I used `r4_harness/power_sim.py` with synthetic date-level ICs, 1,490 development dates, a five-day return horizon (four NW lags), AR(1)=0.40, and 5,000 Monte Carlo draws. With the default 30-stock null IC SD `1/sqrt(29)=0.1857`, effective N is **786.9 dates**, the 80% MDE is **0.01881**, and t≥2 detection probability is **74.4% at IC 0.0183** and **95.6% at IC 0.026**. Because the default assumes independent cross-sectional ranks, I also used a conservative IC SD of 0.30 to stress correlated Dow names: MDE rises to **0.03039**, with power **39.9% at 0.0183** and **66.7% at 0.026**. The 0.0183 threshold is therefore not robustly powered in the Dow. An idealized 500-stock run using the default `1/sqrt(499)` SD gives MDE 0.00453 and power 100% at both planted effects, but that is an optimistic ceiling: it assumes the cross-section behaves like independent assets and does not model industry/graph clusters. Do not count it as a gate pass until the actual point-in-time universe and a conservative cluster SD are established. At 0.0183 IC, the shared top-5 cost case nets only 1.6 bp/week after full replacement, versus 9.1 bp/week at the 50% turnover cap.

**(d) Free-data feasibility.** The two-month pull above verifies that the consolidated FINRA short-volume file is easy to download and contains every stock in the pinned Dow roster during recent development dates. A 2010 file exists with 738 rows, but only its row count was checked; no symbol coverage is claimed for 2010. The 43 recent files are under `r6_data/finra_sample/regsho/`; `regsho_manifest.csv` records date, row count and Dow-ticker coverage. The signal can be formed from reported short and total volume, but the reported total is only the FINRA-reporting facility's denominator, not consolidated market volume. The numerator/denominator must use identical coverage. A point-in-time S&P 500 development pull remains to be checked before relying on wider-universe power.

**(e) Novelty.** Testing whether a fixed, predeclared long-run FINRA short-volume measure predicts next-week downside in today's Dow is a post-public-disclosure replication, not a novel pressure measure. A credible R6 contribution would need to show that FINRA-reported selling pressure predicts returns after conditioning on own prior returns, market/industry exposure and a placebo pressure series, while preserving its sign and paying costs. This should not be dressed up as network diffusion unless a second, independently sourced link predicts incremental peer response. The paper's null result for abnormal short-term flow rules out a loose “daily short spike” story.

## 2. Short-selling pressure propagating from a source firm to linked customers/suppliers

**Gate: FAIL-GATE.**

**(a) Verified evidence.** Wang, Yan and Zheng (2020, cited above) support persistent shorting as a source pressure measure in 2010–2015. Antón and Polk, [“Connected Stocks,” *Journal of Finance* 69 (2014), 1099–1127](https://doi.org/10.1111/jofi.12149), show that common active-fund ownership links stocks and supports a cross-stock reversal mechanism; their graph is ownership-based, not an industry graph. Cohen and Frazzini, [“Economic Links and Predictable Returns,” *Journal of Finance* 63 (2008), 1977–2010](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-6261.2008.01379.x), show customer news predicts supplier returns with a lag. Neither paper establishes that short-sale pressure itself flows along supplier links; this proposal joins separately supported mechanisms and must test that incremental link directly.

**(b) Mega-cap survival.** Customer/supplier economic exposure remains relevant for large firms, and informed short flow could reveal adverse information before linked firms fully react. This is a plausible reason to test a network term in liquid stocks. Counterarguments are strong: Dow megacaps are heavily covered, supply-chain connections are widely known, and linked peers may react simultaneously through ETFs and analyst coverage. The observed effect could be ordinary industry/news momentum, not pressure transmission. The full R6 test must compare source short-flow signal with the same linked-neighbor response after controlling for the peer's own short flow, sector return and public source return.

**(c) Cluster-aware power and cost.** I used the harness's event mode with 30 assets, 1,490 dates, a five-day horizon/four NW lags, AR(1)=0.40, an assumed 0.05 eligible source-event rate per day, negative-binomial event-cluster shape 0.30 and a planted mean event IC of 0.026. This gives about **67.3 active event dates**, **74.4 source events**, **35.5 effective date clusters**, an 80% MDE of **0.0842**, and only **19.2%** power to detect 0.026. This model is already a simplified optimistic design: it treats events on the same date as averaged observations and does not add dependence among stocks sharing one customer/supplier cluster. A top-5 weekly portfolio still needs 11.6–16.5 bp gross per week across the stated IC range; cost is 2.5 bp/week at 50% total turnover but 10 bp/week at full replacement. The event sample does not support a portfolio test at a plausible IC in this six-year window.

**(d) Free-data feasibility.** FINRA short pressure is free and recent Dow coverage was confirmed. Customer/supplier links can in principle be drawn from public SEC filings, but no SEC link pull or 30-name coverage count was completed; actual project coverage, reporting delays and link stability are unknown. Do not assume all 30 stocks have usable directed links or count each peer as an independent event. Data feasibility fails until a no-outcome pull establishes dated link coverage and enough independent source/date clusters. The 2010 row count check does not establish historic link coverage.

**(e) Novelty.** The proposed new element is a source-flow event study: does a source company's persistent net short-sale pressure lead negative peer returns specifically along pre-existing customer/supplier edges, with shuffled-link and reversed-edge placebos? That differs from testing own-stock short-sale predictability or returns after customer news. If links are replaced by a broad sector dummy, it is not a network test. If flow predicts only the source, it collapses to direction 1 and the network claim fails.

## 3. OTC non-ATS weekly activity as retail buying/selling pressure

**Gate: FAIL-GATE; do not use this field as signed retail pressure.**

**(a) Verified evidence.** Boehmer, Jones, Zhang and Zhang, [“Tracking Retail Investor Activity,” *Journal of Finance* 76 (2021), 2249–2305](https://doi.org/10.1111/jofi.13033), find that their marketable retail buy/sell imbalance predicts about 10 bp of next-week return spread. But they classify individual transaction prints from TAQ/FINRA TRF records using sub-penny price improvement: price fraction below a penny signals a retail buy, above a penny signals a retail sell. They use 2010–2015 stock transaction data; they do **not** use FINRA's weekly OTC non-ATS firm/security aggregate. Kelley and Tetlock, [“How Wise Are Crowds?,” *Journal of Finance* 68 (2013), 1229–1265](https://doi.org/10.1111/jofi.12028), also find retail buy/sell order imbalances predict returns using a separate retail-order data source. These papers support directionally signed retail flow, but neither validates weekly non-ATS volume as that measure.

**(b) Mega-cap survival.** Retail marketable activity remains present in large liquid shares, but the cited papers' mechanisms depend on identifying buy versus sell imbalances. The proposed weekly FINRA non-ATS data do not label that side, and “non-ATS firm” is a trading venue/reporting category, not “retail investor.” The effect could be wholesaler inventory, institutional internalization, dealer hedging or other activity. No credible survival case for a **directional** proxy follows from the published retail-flow evidence.

**(c) Cluster-aware power and cost.** For illustration only, I ran the harness at 30 assets and **307 weekly dates** (about six years), AR(1)=0.40 with a conservative four-week HAC lag, and 5,000 simulations. Effective N is **162.1 weeks**, MDE is **0.04144 IC**, and power is **27.0% at 0.0183** and **43.5% at 0.026**. Weekly samples cannot manufacture date clusters by multiplying 30 stocks. The same top-5 weekly cost case is 2.5 bp per rebalance at 50% total turnover, 10 bp at full replacement, against only 11.6–16.5 bp gross at the stated IC range. Since this flow has no signed buying/selling signal, there is no valid direction-specific IC estimate or portfolio edge to price; the calculation shows that even a hypothetical correctly signed version would be underpowered at the lower IC hurdle.

**(d) Free-data feasibility.** Two weeks of raw FINRA weekly summary files cover all 30 project Dow names: 459 and 461 symbol-firm rows. The file exposes week start, symbol, firm identity, share quantity and trade count, but no buyer/seller direction or customer/retail capacity. FINRA's weekly reporting began in 2016, so the series cannot cover 2010; the API's documented historic file is rolling, so a full 2016–2020 history is not established by this pull. The response files are `r6_data/finra_sample/otc_weekly/FINRA_OTC_nonATS_week_20260824.json` and `...20260831.json`. Thus the raw data are free and downloadable, but the required signal is not present.

**(e) Novelty.** Calling weekly OTC non-ATS share quantity “retail pressure” would repeat neither Boehmer et al.'s data nor their design; it would mislabel an unsigned venue-level activity total as an investor-side imbalance. A defensible different question could use *total off-exchange participation* only as a predeclared moderator of a separately measured signed Reg SHO signal. That would be an activity-conditioned short-flow test, not a retail buying-pressure strategy, and it would still need a power and link-coverage pass before consuming a variant.

## Recommendation

No candidate clears the R6 idea gate as written. Keep direction 1 at the top of the backlog only if the team accepts a strict distinction between daily short-sale volume and net bearish pressure and can use a conservative IC dispersion with the 500-name universe. Do not run an outcome test for the network variant until free customer/supplier link coverage and independent event counts pass a no-outcome feasibility gate. Reject the OTC-as-retail-pressure proposal unless a separate signed trade-level dataset is identified; the weekly FINRA non-ATS files alone cannot support the claimed sign.
