# Round U — Cross-asset pressure into Dow stocks

**Scope:** three free-data candidates only; development-window input availability, no equity prices/returns, no signal outcomes, and no holdout data were examined. The only computations below are input row/date counts and design-only simulations from `r4_harness/power_sim.py`.

## Ranking by expected edge (not by ease)

| Rank | Direction | Gate verdict | Subjective chance of meeting the full fixed R6 criteria | Main reason |
|---:|---|---|---:|---|
| 1 | Commodity-futures pressure × issuer exposure | **FAIL-GATE** | 0.5% | Best direct economic link and some firm/peer evidence, but existing studies identify commodity shocks rather than this tradable pressure signal; projected mechanism power is low. |
| 2 | CFTC COT hedging/speculative pressure × issuer exposure | **FAIL-GATE** | 0.2% | Free, dense position data and real hedging-pressure literature; equity mapping and mega-cap post-2020 persistence are not established, and the conservative power scenario misses 80%. |
| 3 | Treasury/credit flow pressure × firm sensitivity | **FAIL-GATE** | <0.1% | Flows exist, but are aggregate and quarterly; the literature mainly identifies bond-yield effects, the usable sample is short, and this is adjacent to earlier Treasury/cash-flow proposals. |

The probabilities are judgment calls for a full pass, including criterion 4 and holdout—not probabilities that an association exists. Decision 76 says a Dow top-five portfolio must plausibly deliver roughly 0.8 annualized excess Sharpe after costs with turnover at or below 20% to be worth advancing. None of the published effects below establishes that result for this Dow design. At the fixed 0.05% per-side cost, a 20% monthly one-way turnover assumption implies about 0.01% cost per rebalance before any additional execution or data frictions; it does not remove the edge-size requirement.

## 1. CFTC COT positions mapped to exposed issuers

**Proposed mechanism.** Use the weekly Disaggregated COT report's producer/merchant and managed-money net positions, each scaled by open interest, as two separately pre-specified measures of commodity-market pressure. Use only reports after their Friday release, matched to the firms' last already-public 10-K/10-Q commodity exposure (producer or material input user), and test whether exposure-weighted pressure predicts next-week issuer-relative returns. This is not the old daily short-sale-volume signal (D1), nor a COT position signal for Treasury futures. Do not choose the trader category after viewing returns.

### Gates (a)–(e)

- **(a) Literature — partial support; gate passes narrowly.** De Roon, Nijman and Veld, [“Hedging Pressure Effects in Futures Markets,” *Journal of Finance* (2000), DOI 10.1111/0022-1082.00253](https://doi.org/10.1111/0022-1082.00253), test 20 futures markets and report own- and cross-market hedging pressure associated with futures returns; they also report explanatory power for returns on the underlying assets. Fan, [“Speculative pressure,” *Journal of Futures Markets* (2020), DOI 10.1002/fut.22085](https://doi.org/10.1002/fut.22085), finds speculative-pressure premia across commodity, currency and equity-index futures. These establish a plausible pressure mechanism in futures, but do not demonstrate a COT-to-individual-equity premium. There is also a measurement warning: [Wang, “Measurement errors in index trader positions data: Is the price pressure hypothesis still invalid?” (2022)](https://doi.org/10.1002/aepp.13186) reports that correcting measurement error did not find a significant true index-trader-position effect for any commodity tested. The sources are verified, but their support is mixed and mostly not stock-level.
- **(b) Mega-cap/post-2010 case — fail.** Commodity producers and large industrial input users can have material commodity exposure, so a cash-flow pathway is economically credible. But these cited COT studies concern futures (and underlying commodity assets), not a persistent issuer-level equity effect in mega-caps after 2020. Large-cap exposure alone does not establish a forecastable cross-sectional return spread.
- **(c) Power — fail at the stated planning effect.** I ran `simulate_power(universe_size=500, n_dates=309, horizon=5, signal_autocorrelation=0.5, cross_sectional_ic_sd=0.10, planted_mean_ic=0.02, simulations=2000, seed=20261111)`. The synthetic probability of NW t≥2 is **64.35%** (Monte Carlo SE 1.07 percentage points); the 80%-power MDE is **0.0241 IC** and mean effective sample size is 138.9. The 0.10 IC standard-deviation assumption is deliberately wider than the independent-rank null for 500 stocks, to allow shared commodity shocks and heterogeneous exposures. It still does not explicitly model dependence across only a few commodity families, so 64.35% is not guaranteed conservative. The mechanism test would be on the date-wise S&P 500 cross-section; its Dow implementation would have far less exposure breadth. The CFTC sample contains 309 distinct report dates over the allowed development window.
- **(d) Free-data feasibility — inputs available; usable issuer panel not established.** I queried the official CFTC public API for metadata/counts only. In March 2021, natural-resources COT contains **868 rows, 5 report dates, and 21 commodity names**; in March 2025, **922 rows, 4 report dates, and 22 commodity names**. Across 2020-11-27–2026-10-30 it contains **67,343 rows, 309 report dates, and 29 commodity names**. No position values were inspected. The CFTC says Disaggregated reports are available from June 2006 and its historical archive has yearly files; reports describe Tuesday positions and are normally published Friday. Sources: [official CFTC historical files](https://www.cftc.gov/MarketReports/CommitmentsofTraders/HistoricalCompressed/index.htm), [COT descriptions/history](https://www.cftc.gov/MarketReports/CommitmentsofTraders/index.htm), [release schedule](https://www.cftc.gov/MarketReports/CommitmentsofTraders/ReleaseSchedule/index.htm), and [public API FAQ](https://publicreporting.cftc.gov/stories/s/Public-Reporting-FAQ/inwp-fmhz/). EDGAR issuer filings are free, but extracting consistent point-in-time quantities and directions of commodity exposure across S&P and Dow issuers is a substantial additional input build; the small Dow exposure cohort is not yet counted. Hence it is not ready for a frozen test.
- **(e) Novelty — plausible, but only if exposure is genuinely issuer-specific.** This differs from D1's own-stock daily short-sale ratio, J1's within-industry reversal, ETF-flow proposals, D6's Treasury-basket trend, and C2's generic Treasury/month-end cash-demand idea. It is also distinct from CFTC measures of commodity futures returns themselves. If exposure is reduced to energy/materials SIC labels, the strategy becomes a few-sector commodity beta and loses the claimed network mechanism.

**Re-entry condition:** first build a development-only point-in-time filing-based exposure coverage table and show enough independent issuer exposure variation; then rerun power with the actual eligible exposure clusters. No variant is justified yet.

## 2. Treasury and credit transaction flows × issuer sensitivity

**Proposed mechanism.** A potentially distinct design would use newly released FRED/Z.1 net transactions in Treasury securities and corporate/foreign bonds, plus public TIC monthly foreign-security transactions, as dated flow shocks. Interact the latest released flow innovation with a pre-fixed issuer sensitivity (for example, published debt maturity/financing dependence or bank-credit dependence), and test next-quarter relative stock returns. Use release dates, not quarter-end labels, to avoid look-ahead. This is not D6's Treasury-basket price trend or a Treasury yield momentum rule.

### Gates (a)–(e)

- **(a) Literature — partial support; gate fails for the proposed stock mechanism.** D'Amico and King, [“Flow and Stock Effects of Large-Scale Treasury Purchases,” Federal Reserve FEDS 2010-52](https://www.federalreserve.gov/pubs/feds/2010/201052/201052pap.pdf), identify purchase-date flow and persistent stock effects on the yields of Treasury maturity sectors. D'Amico, King and/or D'Amico and King’s later security-level follow-up, [*Journal of Financial Economics* (2013), DOI 10.1016/j.jfineco.2012.11.007](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jfineco.2012.11.007), also documents local supply effects in Treasury yields. Phillot, [“US Treasury Auctions: A High-Frequency Identification of Supply Shocks,” *AEJ: Macroeconomics* (2025), DOI 10.1257/mac.20210243](https://doi.org/10.1257/mac.20210243), finds supply surprises move stock prices, volatility and corporate bond yields in the aggregate. These support security-market transmission, but not a persistent stock-level ranking based on Z.1/TIC flow data. The literature's security-level identifying variation is not present in the aggregate FRED series.
- **(b) Mega-cap/post-2010 case — fail.** The 2025 auction study includes supply shocks through 2020 and reports aggregate stock-price effects, which is relevant to large liquid markets, but it does not establish persistence after 2020 or cross-sectional differences among mega-caps. The older QE studies concern a policy-purchase episode and Treasury yields. The case does not justify a Dow exposure ranking.
- **(c) Power — fail.** The FRED data used here are quarterly, so the design's conservative non-overlapping sample is **22** usable quarterly observations from 2021-01-01 through 2026-04-01 (after excluding the 2020-10-01 quarter label that precedes the development start). Using `universe_size=500, n_dates=22, horizon=1, signal_autocorrelation=0.5, cross_sectional_ic_sd=0.10, planted_mean_ic=0.02, simulations=2000, seed=20261111`, the synthetic probability of NW t≥2 is **29.10%** (Monte Carlo SE 1.02 points); the 80%-power MDE is **0.0606 IC**. This models only date-level noise, so repeated/lagged releases and common shocks may reduce effective information further. Monthly interpolation would not create independent observations.
- **(d) Free-data feasibility — series exist, but fail for the intended cross-section.** I pulled date rows only from FRED's public CSV endpoint. Treasury transactions [BOGZ1FU893061105Q](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/BOGZ1FU893061105Q) and corporate/foreign-bond transactions [BOGZ1FU893063005Q](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/BOGZ1FU893063005Q) each have **22** dated observations within 2020-11-27–2026-10-30 (first 2021-01-01; last 2026-04-01). I did not inspect values. Official [TIC monthly transaction documentation](https://home.treasury.gov/data/treasury-international-capital-tic-system) confirms monthly aggregate flows across broad security types; [FRED Z.1 tables](https://www.federalreserve.gov/releases/z1/) are quarterly. These aggregate series do not name recipient Dow issuers or identify which firm receives Treasury/credit demand. A point-in-time firm sensitivity panel would need additional accounting/credit disclosures and would still not make aggregate purchases firm-specific. The macro source is free; the required exposure-and-transmission inputs are not yet feasible as a clean free panel.
- **(e) Novelty — weak and adjacent.** The distinction from E/D6 is transaction flows rather than Treasury prices/yields, and from C2 is security-purchase supply rather than month-end cash settlement. But C2 already proposed Treasury cash-demand pressure with a sector response, and E/D6 tested Treasury basket trend. Without security-level issuer flows, the surviving signal is a macro liquidity/credit factor. That is too close to rejected macro/Treasury designs, and its shared time-series shock is not a convincing related-asset network.

**Verdict:** dead under the current free-data and fixed-universe rules. Better data could make an event study possible, but it would be a different licensed/security-level project, not an R6 direction to advance now.

## 3. Daily commodity-futures trading pressure × exposed Dow names

**Proposed mechanism.** For each preselected liquid contract (e.g., WTI, natural gas, copper, gold), measure a one-month futures trading-pressure proxy from daily futures OHLCV (a pre-specified signed close-location × volume measure, normalized within contract). Interact that market-level pressure with each issuer's most recent public producer/input exposure. The sign must differ for producers and cost-sensitive users, fixed before any equity outcomes; then estimate one date-wise cross-sectional S&P 500 IC and only afterward test a Dow implementation. This is distinct from COT positioning: it uses realized daily futures trading, not trader-category positions.

### Gates (a)–(e)

- **(a) Literature — passes for economic transmission, not the exact signal.** Kilian and Park, [“The Impact of Oil Price Shocks on the U.S. Stock Market,” *International Economic Review* (2009), DOI 10.1111/j.1468-2354.2009.00568.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1468-2354.2009.00568.x), show industry returns respond differently depending on oil supply versus demand shocks. Crawford, Markarian, Muslu and Price, [“Oil prices, earnings, and stock returns,” *Review of Accounting Studies* (2021), DOI 10.1007/s11142-020-09556-7](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11142-020-09556-7), find oil-price effects on earnings/investments vary by firm, industry and producer/consumer status, and that responses to oil-related earnings spill over to industry peers. Hwang and Kim, [“Oil price shocks and the US stock market: A nonlinear approach,” *Journal of Empirical Finance* (2021), DOI 10.1016/j.jempfin.2021.08.004](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jempfin.2021.08.004), find state-dependent and sector-varying responses, especially for auto versus energy firms. Those papers support the exposure pathway, but their shock variables are not a daily OHLCV pressure signal.
- **(b) Mega-cap/post-2010 case — fail as a persistence claim.** The 2021 firm-level oil/earnings evidence is closer to large issuers than small-stock microstructure work, and the literature reports heterogeneous producer/consumer effects. Still, publication in 2021 is not proof of persistence after 2020; the cited work does not establish that this exact pressure signal predicts post-2020 mega-cap returns or survives in the current Dow. The oil shock sign itself is state-dependent, not a universal buy/sell sign. A frozen proposal would need a single pre-registered commodity and exposure direction rather than pooled, sign-selected commodities.
- **(c) Power — fail.** I used a monthly 21-session design over 71 development formation dates, with S&P 500 n=500, conservative cross-sectional IC SD 0.10, serial correlation 0.5, planted IC 0.02, 2,000 simulations, seed 20261111. `power_sim` gives **30.45%** for NW t≥2 (Monte Carlo SE 1.03 points), effective n≈25.3 and 80%-power MDE **0.0565 IC** at NW lag 20. Because all firms share a small number of commodity shocks, this setup may still overstate power. The portfolio feasibility rule remains the decision-76 hurdle—roughly 0.8 annualized Dow excess Sharpe after costs at ≤20% turnover—and none of the papers reports that statistic for a top-five Dow book.
- **(d) Free-data feasibility — daily timestamps pass; robust input construction does not.** For a cheap metadata-only sample check, Yahoo Finance's free continuous-futures chart endpoint returned **1,493 daily timestamps each** for `CL=F`, `NG=F`, `HG=F`, and `GC=F`, spanning 2020-11-27 to 2026-10-30. I counted timestamps only and did not inspect OHLCV values or construct a pressure series. This establishes that a public proxy can be fetched; it does not validate volume, contract roll timing, back-adjustment, or point-in-time availability. The endpoint is not an exchange-certified historical contract tape and may revise its continuous series. A firm exposure panel still needs point-in-time public filings; use of crude oil alone also leaves a very small and heterogeneous Dow cohort. Sources for the underlying economic pathway include the papers above; the sample endpoint is [Yahoo Finance chart API](https://query1.finance.yahoo.com/v8/finance/chart/CL%3DF?range=5y&interval=1d) and is not a guarantee of licensed or archival quality.
- **(e) Novelty — distinct data, but possible overlap in hypothesis.** Compared with the rejected ETF-to-constituent proposal H/O2, this would use commodity futures rather than a sector ETF; compared with COT it would use realized daily pressure rather than position snapshots; compared with old commodity/industry work it would test a pre-specified exposed-stock cross-section. It must not collapse to a generic oil-price beta, commodity momentum, or ETF-flow strategy. That novelty is real but narrow and does not compensate for power and coverage gaps.

**Verdict:** fail at present. Buildable as a rough Yahoo prototype on free data, but not yet a defensible point-in-time futures-pressure × issuer-exposure dataset. No signal, equity outcome, or price-return relationship was calculated in this screen.

## What was checked and what was not

- CFTC API: counts and distinct dates/commodity names only, for the development range; no position magnitudes or derived pressure.
- FRED CSV: date rows only for Treasury-security and corporate/foreign-bond transactions; no flow values, revisions, or return analysis.
- Yahoo futures chart API: timestamp counts only for four contracts; no OHLCV values or derived signal.
- `power_sim.py`: synthetic, assumption-driven NW-t≥2 design calculations only; not power estimated from project outcomes.
- No Dow/S&P stock prices, forward returns, ICs, Sharpe ratios, or portfolio outcomes were accessed or computed. No holdout files or data were opened.
