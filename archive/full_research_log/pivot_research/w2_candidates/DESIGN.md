# Pivot design and evidence review

## Recommendation

The fixed in-sample selection rule below chooses a 63-session low-volatility tilt. It is the best of six new candidates by annualized return relative to EW30 in IS (+5.58 percentage points), but the paired daily active-return test is weak (Newey–West HAC, 10 lags: t = 0.57, two-sided p = 0.57). It then trails EW30 in both held-out windows (−7.72 pp in OOS1 and −12.26 pp in OOS2). The result is therefore an IS-selected hypothesis that failed this OOS check, not demonstrated outperformance. No candidate has a positive OOS excess return in both windows.

The pivot specification is recorded so it can be evaluated prospectively on data after the freeze date. Do not retune it from the OOS results below. If it also fails future evaluation, use EW30 as the reference implementation and report that daily OHLCV did not establish a reliable active edge in this 30-stock universe.

## What the literature says

**Short-term reversal.** Jegadeesh (1990) documents negative first-order serial correlation at the monthly horizon, and Lehmann (1990) reports weekly winner–loser reversals that he attributes partly to liquidity around large price moves. These are not a blank cheque for a five-session rule: bid–ask bounce can create apparent reversal in transaction-price data, and daily OHLC bars do not reveal the spread or quote midpoint needed to separate that mechanism cleanly. Short-horizon reversal also trades often. It can survive costs in some markets and designs, especially with a buy/hold buffer, but gross historical evidence is not proof that a concentrated weekly Dow strategy nets a premium. In our first-round screen, weekly reversal lost in both OOS periods; 21-day reversal won both OOS periods but lost IS, so it would not have been selected under the fixed IS rule.

**Industry and peer effects / lead–lag.** Moskowitz and Grinblatt (1999) find industry momentum, while Hou (2007) links lead–lag effects to slow diffusion of industry information, particularly between large and small firms. Cohen and Frazzini (2008) use actual customer–supplier links to identify delayed responses to economically related firms. Those links are not present in OHLCV, and a 30-stock correlation graph is only a noisy proxy for economic peers. Lower-turnover peer signals may be more cost-tolerant than weekly contrarian trades, but the present round-one network results were small and statistically weak (reported p-values around 0.09–0.10); another round-one engine disagreed on signs. We cannot claim a robust, implementable peer premium from these results.

**Low volatility and betting against beta.** Baker, Bradley and Wurgler (2011) document the low-volatility / low-beta effect and discuss benchmark constraints and demand for lottery-like risky stocks as possible explanations. Frazzini and Pedersen (2014) construct a long–short betting-against-beta factor: long leveraged low-beta assets and short high-beta assets. A low-volatility long-only tilt is easier to implement in this project than BAB, but it is not the same trade: this study has no leverage, borrow, financing, or reliable beta-risk model, and 63-day beta estimates on 30 names are noisy. Low-risk portfolios often trade less than weekly reversal, helping costs, but low turnover cannot guarantee a premium. In this study both 63-day low volatility and low beta were positive only in IS and negative in both OOS periods.

**Residual momentum.** Blitz, Huij and Martens (2011) show that ranking on factor-residual returns can improve on conventional momentum in their broader stock sample and can reduce factor exposures. Their result uses a longer-horizon momentum design; it does not imply that a five-day residual signal or a 30-stock, OHLCV-only proxy works. Longer-horizon residual momentum generally trades less than weekly signals and may therefore better tolerate costs, but a long–short factor implementation has financing and shorting assumptions absent here. The round-one residual-momentum candidate was negative in all three periods. Any post-publication or out-of-sample expectation should also be discounted: McLean and Pontiff (2016) estimate average return declines of 26% out of sample and 58% after publication across 97 published predictors. Those broad averages are not a forecast for this strategy, but they argue against treating published anomalies as unchanged future returns.

**Volatility timing.** Moreira and Muir (2017) find that volatility-managed factor portfolios can improve Sharpe ratios by reducing exposure when realized volatility is high. That evidence concerns diversified factor portfolios and is not automatically transferable to a small long-only stock basket. Timing on a short rolling window can be noisy; costs depend on how often exposure changes, and cash returns matter. Our fixed EW volatility-timing candidate was slightly negative versus EW30 in all three windows (closest in OOS1 at −0.50 pp), with no significant active-return result.

Across anomaly research, transaction costs reduce returns and statistical strength. Novy-Marx and Velikov (2016) find that buy/hold spreads are useful for cost control and that few high-turnover strategies retain significant net spreads in their broad study. Their turnover convention and universe differ from ours, so this is a design warning rather than a direct threshold. The official strategy’s roughly 71% name replacement per rebalance and about 3.6% annual cost drag make this concern concrete. The candidate harness applies a 5 bp per-side cost to actual traded notional; flat costs still omit market impact, spread variation, taxes, and borrow.

## Six new candidates

All six definitions were fixed before running the new screen. Each uses the same 30-stock point-in-time roster, five-session rebalance grid, top-five equal-weight long book, top-eight hold buffer, next-session open fills, and 5 bp per-side cost on actual traded notional. IS and OOS1 use the 2020-08-31 roster; OOS2 uses the 2024-11-08 roster. EW30 uses the same grid, fills, cost convention and terminal liquidation. Excess is the difference in annualized geometric return versus EW30, in percentage points. Sharpe is annualized daily Sharpe with zero risk-free rate. Turnover is average traded notional divided by pretrade equity per rebalance (including drift and both buys and sells), not the percentage of names changed. HAC t and p test mean paired daily active return with 10 Newey–West lags; p-values are unadjusted across trials.

The harness runs IS and OOS1 continuously from the 2020-11-27 anchor, so OOS1 inherits the holdings and costs created by the same rebalance grid; OOS2 uses its own 2024-12-06 anchor and roster. The EW30 audit is close to the reported baseline: annualized net return / Sharpe is 6.74% / 0.48 in IS, 17.89% / 1.51 in OOS1, and 11.26% / 0.75 in OOS2, versus project figures of about 6.9% / 0.50, 17.9% / 1.55, and 11.3% / 0.75. This is a simplified independent harness; small differences remain in daily accounting and timing.

Only the first two candidates have positive IS excess; both lose to EW30 in both OOS windows. Thus low volatility is the IS winner under the stated rule, not a robust all-period winner. No OOS-only positive candidate was found in this screen. The large negative results for the reversal combinations reflect both weak ranking performance and high turnover/costs. HAC results are descriptive and not corrected for the six comparisons; crucially, neither IS winner is close to a positive significance claim.

Definitions:

1. **63-session low volatility:** rank stocks from lowest to highest annualized sample standard deviation of 63 adjusted close-to-close daily returns; choose the five lowest.
2. **63-session low beta:** estimate each name’s OLS beta over the last 63 daily returns against the equal-weight daily return of the roster; choose the five lowest betas.
3. **Volatility-scaled residual reversal:** rank on the negative five-session stock return minus equal-weight roster return, divided by the stock’s 63-session annualized volatility scaled by √(5/252); choose the five highest scores.
4. **Low-volume reversal:** rank the negative five-session return multiplied by max(0, 1 − mean volume over five sessions / mean volume over 25 sessions); choose the five highest scores.
5. **Residual reversal + low volatility:** average the cross-sectional percentile ranks of negative five-session market-residual return and negative 63-session volatility; choose the five highest.
6. **Volatility-timed EW30:** hold all 30 names equally, with gross exposure min(100%, 15% / lagged 21-session annualized EW30 volatility); residual portfolio value is cash earning 0%. The volatility estimate ends before the signal session.

The full per-period output, including paired HAC statistics, is in [`candidate_results.csv`](candidate_results.csv); the reproducible screen is [`candidate_harness.py`](candidate_harness.py). The new screen contains six strategy variants (18 strategy-period evaluations). Adding these to the approximately 44 distinct round-one variants gives approximately **50 strategy variants** in total; benchmarks are not counted as candidates. Round-one engines partly overlap and sometimes disagree, so 50 is a transparent trial inventory, not 50 independent hypotheses.

## Frozen pivot specification and selection rule

**Selection rule.** Compare the six named candidates above using IS dates only (2020-11-27 through 2022-12-30), under the common implementation and cost assumptions. Select the candidate with the highest annualized geometric return in excess of EW30. If all six have negative IS excess, select EW30. Break an exact tie using the higher IS Sharpe, then lower IS traded notional. The rule selects the 63-session low-volatility tilt. OOS1 and OOS2 are reported once as validation and do not change the selected parameters.

**Portfolio.** At each rebalance, use the current point-in-time Dow 30 roster and compute each name’s sample standard deviation of adjusted close-to-close returns over the 63 most recent completed sessions. Rank from lowest volatility to highest. Hold five names at 20% each. Keep an existing name while it ranks within the top eight; fill vacancies with the highest-ranked names not already held until five positions are held. Sell held names ranking below eight. No shorts, leverage, sector constraints, discretionary overrides, or volatility forecast clipping are used.

**Timing and costs.** Calculate the signal after each fifth market close, starting from the stated anchor. Execute all buys and sells at the next session’s adjusted open, then mark at adjusted closes. Charge 0.05% on each dollar of actual buy and sell notional, including drift in retained positions, the initial purchase, and final liquidation. Use the same costs, rebalance dates and execution convention for EW30. Cash earns zero. The fixed windows and rosters are IS 2020-11-27–2022-12-30 and OOS1 2023-01-03–2024-02-23 on the 2020-08-31 roster, and OOS2 2024-12-06–2025-12-31 on the 2024-11-08 roster.

**Evaluation and fallback.** Report absolute annualized return, zero-rate Sharpe, maximum drawdown, traded notional, excess return versus EW30, and paired daily active-return inference. Use a 10-session Newey–West HAC standard error and also show a moving-block bootstrap interval if the group’s full evaluation engine is available. Do not claim outperformance unless the net excess is statistically distinguishable from zero and survives the stated costs; account for all 50 trials when discussing selection bias. If the pivot loses to EW30 in the future OOS evaluation, or its excess is not statistically distinguishable from zero, retain EW30 as the transparent baseline and conclude that the OHLCV signals tested did not establish an active premium. Do not switch to a different candidate because it happened to perform well in a previously inspected OOS window.
