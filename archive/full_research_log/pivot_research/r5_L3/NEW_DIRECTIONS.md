# R5-L3: Two additional network-pressure directions

**Purpose and limits.** These are research candidates, not pre-registrations. They use free issuer filings, public index announcements and the project Dow development OHLCV. No new backtests or outcome calculations were run; the specified D5 mechanism report was read only for status context, and no holdout inputs were accessed. I could not find `r4_L2/BUILDABLE.md` in the workspace, so I used the available L2 synthesis/citation audit, L3 buildable and fallback notes, L1 postmortem, D4/D5/D6 dossiers, D5 mechanism results, and the decision brief. Candidate rank is about mechanism and input plausibility, not observed performance.

## Rank 1 — Customer earnings pressure through named customer–supplier links

**Mechanism and prediction.** A material customer’s earnings release can convey demand news about a named supplier. A positive market reaction in the customer should predict same-sign supplier returns after the release as investors update the linked supplier more slowly; negative customer reactions predict negative supplier returns. Test the network effect over the supplier’s next five-session open-to-close return window, after the customer event response is public. This is a price-response proxy for information-linked buying pressure, not direct signed order flow. Cohen and Frazzini find cross-prediction between economically linked firms using principal-customer relationships; Menzly and Ozbas find return cross-predictability across supplier/customer industries. Foster and Ramnath document announcement-related information transfers. These papers motivate, but do not establish, a daily five-session Dow effect.

**Network and free inputs.** Use directed edges `customer → supplier` only when the supplier’s public SEC 10-K or 10-Q explicitly names the customer. Give each verified relationship unit weight; do not infer links from SIC or anonymized “Customer A” disclosures. Extract the first public appearance and most recent reaffirmation date from EDGAR filing text; a link becomes usable on the first NYSE session after the filing date and is carried forward until a later filing changes or removes it. Use SEC Form 8-K Item 2.02 filings and attached releases to identify customer earnings events. Verify the item and actual release date in the filed document; use the first complete regular session after the later of release time and EDGAR acceptance; if the exhibit lacks a release timestamp, use the first session strictly after the filing date. At that session’s close, define the customer shock as its adjusted close-to-close return minus SPY’s return. For supplier `j`, score the sum of event shocks from its named customers, divided by the count of currently valid named customer links. Inputs are free SEC EDGAR filings and submissions metadata, point-in-time Dow rosters, and daily development OHLCV. SEC-hosted APIs and filings are public; [Form 8-K Item 2.02](https://www.sec.gov/forms/form8-k.pdf) describes the results announcement disclosure and required exhibit. The link history requires manual/parsed issuer matching; record SEC CIK, ticker history, filing accession and date for every edge.

**Placebo and input gate.** Before outcomes, count distinct customer–supplier pairs, customer event dates, and events with at least one active Dow supplier recipient. If fewer than **30 distinct event dates** have at least one valid Dow supplier, declare the design infeasible. Do not replace named links with SIC peers. The primary placebo reassigns supplier endpoints among Dow firms matched on the frozen two-digit SIC and market-cap bin, preserving each customer’s out-degree and event date; compare the observed five-session IC with this randomized-link distribution. Also report reversed supplier-to-customer edges as a directional falsification. A similar placebo response means the economic network interpretation fails.

**Turnover and hurdle.** The mechanism test itself is event-based. A portfolio, if later authorized after a mechanism pass, would update only after newly public customer events, so calendar-average turnover may be lower than weekly D4/D5 trading; simultaneous events and changing top-ranked suppliers could still create sharp event-day turnover. Do not claim a turnover number before constructing the signal. The project’s **0.0183 mean-IC hurdle** is a top-five weekly cost reference, not a universal conversion for event-driven signals; nevertheless, given a small named-link graph and five-session window, I do not expect this design to clear that hurdle or costs without unusually broad link/event coverage. Report actual event-portfolio turnover and costs before making any tradability claim.

**Failure modes and distinction.** Major-customer names may be omitted, masked, or disclosed inconsistently; filer/customer CIK and ticker histories need careful matching; one customer can trigger overlapping suppliers’ events; earnings news is information, not necessarily non-informational buying pressure; and large, well-followed Dow firms may reprice immediately. This differs from D4’s daily CLV on a common-owner graph because the shock is a timestamped customer earnings response and links are explicit supply-chain contracts. It differs from D5’s volume-ranked within-SIC leader because no SIC peer proxy or trading-volume leader creates an edge. It differs from D6’s monthly equity–bond return trend because it is a daily stock-to-stock event on an operating relationship.

**Verified literature.** [Cohen & Frazzini (2008), *Economic Links and Predictable Returns*, Journal of Finance, DOI 10.1111/j.1540-6261.2008.01379.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-6261.2008.01379.x); [Menzly & Ozbas (2010), *Market Segmentation and Cross-predictability of Returns*, Journal of Finance, DOI 10.1111/j.1540-6261.2010.01578.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-6261.2010.01578.x); [Foster (1981), *Intra-industry Information Transfers Associated with Earnings Releases*, Journal of Accounting and Economics, DOI 10.1016/0165-4101(81)90003-3](https://doi.org/10.1016/0165-4101(81)90003-3); [Ramnath (2002), *Investor and Analyst Reactions to Earnings Announcements of Related Firms*, Journal of Accounting Research, DOI 10.1111/1475-679X.t01-1-00057](https://doi.org/10.1111/1475-679X.t01-1-00057). Publisher/author pages and abstracts were opened for Cohen–Frazzini, Menzly–Ozbas and Ramnath; the Foster record was verified through the journal search result and DOI. The empirical evidence is mainly broader-market and longer-horizon; none validates this exact Dow setup.

## Rank 2 — Index-change pressure transmitted through common-owner peers

**Mechanism and prediction.** An official Dow component addition creates mechanical demand, while a deletion creates mechanical supply. If prices are affected by demand and connected holders rebalance or transmit demand across related holdings, a positive announcement-session abnormal return in an added source (or negative response in a deleted source) may predict same-sign short-horizon returns among its common-owner Dow peers. The sign is continuation for the source shock; the network-specific claim is that the response is stronger for highly connected recipients. Shleifer and Harris–Gurel document price pressure around index inclusion; Polonchek and Krehbiel study price and volume responses to Dow Average roster changes. Antón and Polk document that common active-fund ownership connects stocks and relates to cross-stock return behavior. These papers do not establish that an index event spills into common-owner peers.

**Network and free inputs.** Build the event calendar from dated S&P Dow Jones Indices public press releases, recording announcement timestamp, effective date, entrant, and departing constituent. A source event is a single official inclusion or deletion announcement concerning a firm in the point-in-time Dow price universe. At the first full regular session after the announcement is public, compute the source’s adjusted close-to-close excess return versus SPY. On that session, score each other active Dow name by the source shock multiplied by its row-normalized cosine common-owner edge from the latest public 13F graph; exclude the source itself and report graph age. The 13F network uses the dated point-in-time manager roster and filed positions only after their filing-date availability; SEC filings, daily OHLCV, public EDGAR metadata, and official S&P DJI releases are free. A public sample announcement shows S&P DJI publishes the change, effective date and both tickers in its release: [2020 Dow change announcement](https://press.spglobal.com/2020-08-24-Salesforce-com-Amgen-and-Honeywell-International-Set-to-Join-Dow-Jones-Industrial-Average). The SEC’s [EDGAR data APIs](https://data.sec.gov/) provide public filing access. This reuses a 13F link source but replaces D4’s continuous CLV signal with a discrete index-demand event.

**Placebo and input gate.** First list all official announcements and count independent source events and event dates from the free announcement archive without joining returns. Require at least **20 distinct source-event dates** in the development window and at least **three active, non-source common-owner recipients per event date**; otherwise declare infeasible before outcome construction. Do not expand to S&P 500 events or non-Dow securities. The primary placebo degree-preserving rewires the 13F graph within each announcement date while holding the event source shock, source/deletion label, recipient roster and degree distribution fixed; the real peer IC must exceed the one-sided placebo 95th percentile. Report source-stock event response separately from peer response so the known inclusion effect cannot be mislabelled as network propagation.

**Turnover and hurdle.** There would be no routine weekly rebalance; positions would update only at registered component announcements and be held for the fixed five-session event horizon. Average calendar-time turnover may therefore be low, but event-date portfolio turnover can be concentrated and high. Do not infer low transaction costs from sparse dates. The **0.0183 IC hurdle** is a conservative comparison point, not a direct cost conversion for episodic events. Given the small number of DJIA changes, thin network breadth and the fact that the literature’s clearest effect is on the added/deleted stock rather than its peers, I consider clearing the peer-IC hurdle unlikely; the input-only gate may already stop the design.

**Failure modes and distinction.** There may be too few distinct Dow changes for meaningful inference; announcements may coincide with earnings or corporate actions; the event is public and anticipated; the common-owner graph may not identify index funds specifically; price pressure may reverse quickly; and reported 13F positions are delayed. This differs from D4 because only a sparse, externally timed index-demand shock is tested, not daily neighbor CLV. It differs from D5 because peer links are common ownership, not SIC membership or dollar-volume-ranked leaders. It differs from D6 because it is single-stock event pressure and same-equity peer response, not monthly cross-asset trend.

**Verified literature.** [Shleifer (1986), *Do Demand Curves for Stocks Slope Down?*, Journal of Finance, DOI 10.1111/j.1540-6261.1986.tb04518.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-6261.1986.tb04518.x); [Harris & Gurel (1986), *Price and Volume Effects Associated with Changes in the S&P 500 List*, Journal of Finance, DOI 10.1111/j.1540-6261.1986.tb04550.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-6261.1986.tb04550.x); [Polonchek & Krehbiel (1994), *Price and Volume Effects Associated with Changes in the Dow Jones Averages*, Quarterly Review of Economics and Finance, DOI 10.1016/1062-9769(94)90016-7](https://doi.org/10.1016/1062-9769(94)90016-7); [Antón & Polk (2014), *Connected Stocks*, Journal of Finance, DOI 10.1111/jofi.12149](https://doi.org/10.1111/jofi.12149). Publisher pages/abstracts were opened for Shleifer and Antón–Polk; publisher search records were checked for Harris–Gurel and Polonchek–Krehbiel. Index event evidence concerns affected stocks, while the common-owner peer-transmission step remains the new hypothesis.

## Ranking and recommendation

Rank one is the named customer–supplier earnings design because it has repeated potential source events, a directly interpretable economic edge, and several verified information-transfer papers. Its named-link coverage within only thirty large stocks is still doubtful, and it is **unlikely** to achieve the 0.0183 mean-IC hurdle after costs. Rank two is the index-change event design because the pressure shock is unusually interpretable, but likely event scarcity and the extra peer-transmission assumption make it the less promising candidate; it is also **unlikely** to clear the hurdle and may be infeasible before outcomes. Neither should be treated as a substitute for the pending D4/D6 decisions. If either is selected, first run the stated input-only gate, then freeze a separate preregistration and register all endpoints before viewing any return outcomes.
