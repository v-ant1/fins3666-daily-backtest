# Red-team review of the three candidate directions

**Scope:** These are the strongest objections and prospective falsification criteria, not test results. No backtests, returns, or outcome data were loaded or computed. Costs refer to the stated 0.05% per side (5 bp per buy or sell) and are not netted against unrun portfolio outcomes.

## Direction 1 — retail marketable-order imbalance

**Strongest case against.** The closest large-cap evidence is already economically small: Boehmer et al.’s largest-cap tercile spread is 2.6 bp per week before costs, while its broader sample is about 10–11 bp. A fully reversed long/short position can incur both a buy and a sell cost (10 bp round trip per leg before turnover scaling), so the large-cap gross spread is not a comfortable cost cushion. More directly, Ardia, Aymard and Cenesizoglu’s 2024 re-examination reports that the 2016–21 signal no longer predicts weekly returns for large-cap stocks and the retail long-short strategy is no longer profitable; it also finds sensitivity to trade-signing method. Dow large caps in 2020–26 are closer to this adverse period and universe than to the original small-stock-heavy evidence. This literature is still a working-paper/arXiv re-examination, but it attacks the exact extrapolation this direction needs.

**Weakest evidence.** The weakest step is carrying an old aggregate cross-sectional result into Dow constituents in 2020–26 after costs. The main supporting study uses TAQ and a retail classification rule; daily OHLCV cannot reproduce the measure. The 2.6 bp result is a highest-cap tercile statistic, not a Dow estimate or demonstrated post-cost return. Whether retail imbalance measures persistent informed demand versus correlated public news remains uncertain.

**Falsifying result.** Freeze the retail-flow construction and sign method before looking at returns. The direction fails if, in the prespecified 2020–26 Dow sample, lagged signed retail imbalance has no positive 1- or 5-day predictive coefficient with confidence intervals excluding a minimally useful positive effect, if signs reverse under the preselected alternate signing rule, or if a same-size market/volume/news baseline performs as well. A portfolio stage is falsifying if gross long-short return is erased by 5 bp/side costs and turnover, or a time-shuffled retail-flow placebo predicts similarly. Test these only after data is available; no such result is reported here.

**L1 postmortem fit.** `r4_L1\POSTMORTEM.md` was checked and is absent as of 10 November 2026, so the root-cause reconciliation is provisional. This direction directly tests measured signed pressure and obeys mechanism-first sequencing, addressing the project concern that a candle proxy is not observed order flow and that portfolios came before mechanism tests. It does not by itself fix the correlation-graph/network issue; it is a direct persistence test, not a network-spread test. It also risks being infeasible without TAQ/retail-trade data.

## Direction 2 — forced mutual-fund flow across common holdings

**Strongest case against.** Classic flow/fire-sale evidence concerns episodic stressed selling and broad historical US universes, not everyday daily signals in 30 current Dow firms. Coval and Stafford’s monthly event study finds forced-sale declines and a 6.13% rebound over the following 12 months (Table 4, t=2.47), but the estimate rests on only 18 quarters with enough extreme forced selling for conservative grouped inference; voluntary selling does not show the same reversal. Lou’s expected-flow result uses quarterly formation/long holding horizons. Neither source shows that a point-in-time flow network will predict one-week Dow returns after 5 bp/side costs. Holdings reported quarterly or with disclosure lags may not reveal when pressure was actually deployed, and mapping fund demand into “common-holder network spread” can confuse shared factor/news exposure with causal transmission. A separate quasi-natural-experiment study of Chilean pension-fund sales (Larrain, Muñoz and Tessada, “Asset Fire Sales in Equity Markets,” *Journal of Financial Intermediation* 30, 2017, DOI 10.1016/j.jfi.2016.06.001) finds a roughly 4% relative loss in the forced-sale month but reports recovery only after four to six months. It is a different market and flow source, not a replication of Coval–Stafford, but cautions against assuming a short 1–20-day reversal horizon.

**Weakest evidence.** The weakest evidence is the proposed daily 1–20-day response and shared-holder network extension: the cited studies establish monthly stock-level flow pressure or quarterly expected flow predictability, not daily network propagation among 30 large caps. The market-level historic long-short figures do not establish current Dow implementation or cost-adjusted profitability, and an independent replication of this exact Dow/common-holder construction was not found. Public 13F data is incomplete, delayed, and unsuitable for observing contemporaneous daily flows.

**Falsifying result.** Freeze point-in-time holdings, flow dates, and the exposure construction. The mechanism fails if identified outflow shocks do not precede negative returns at exposed holdings, followed by positive post-event returns at the specified 1/5/20-day or monthly horizons; if pre-event coefficients show the same pattern; or if sector/market matched controls and shuffled ownership links produce comparable responses. The network-propagation claim specifically fails if high shared-flow exposure has no incremental predictive content over each stock’s own pressure and common sector/market returns. A future portfolio stage fails if measured expected gross return cannot cover turnover at 5 bp/side. None of these tests has been run.

**L1 postmortem fit.** `r4_L1\POSTMORTEM.md` is absent, so this reconciliation remains provisional. Of the three directions, this most directly replaces the original return-correlation graph with an economic exposure network, and it follows the requirement to establish a flow mechanism before a portfolio. It also targets the likely gaps around directionality and thesis drift. It does not fix limited cross-sectional breadth or stale holdings; if L1 identifies those as root causes, this is only a partial remedy.

## Direction 3 — ETF creations/redemptions and constituent arbitrage pressure

**Strongest case against.** Ben-David, Franzoni and Moussawi document an ETF arbitrage/ownership channel in 2000–15 and published it in 2018; the work shows higher volatility and subsequent price reversals, not a guaranteed directional trading edge in 2020–26. Their results are based on ETF ownership and flow/mispricing measures across much wider US samples. ETF ownership alone is not a flow shock, and contemporaneous creations, redemptions, holdings, and NAV mispricing may be difficult to obtain cleanly for each Dow member. Greenwood and Sammon show that the classic S&P index addition/deletion effect had faded to statistically insignificant values by 2010–20 (NBER WP 30748), warning that observable demand shocks may be arbitraged quickly. The Dow’s 30 names may also have too little variation in relevant ETF ownership to identify the hypothesized scaling.

**Weakest evidence.** The weakest step is the proposed signed flow-to-Dow mapping and net reversal portfolio. The anchor paper’s volatility result is stronger than the evidence for a tradable return forecast, and its sample predates most of the target period. S&P 500 index additions/deletions are only adjacent evidence, not direct evidence about daily ETF creation/redemption shocks. Reported flow-response estimates are gross and are not calibrated against 5 bp/side costs or turnover.

**Falsifying result.** Freeze the ETF flow source, holdings timestamp, and signed flow construction. The mechanism fails if high ownership does not amplify same-day constituent response to an independently measured creation/redemption shock, if the specified 1–20-day reversal has the wrong sign or no incremental content after same-day return/volume and common factors, or if shuffled ETF-to-stock links yield similar estimates. It fails as a portfolio candidate if the event signal is too sparse, exposures have little cross-sectional variation, or the prespecified long-short fade does not cover 5 bp/side costs and turnover. No test has been run.

**L1 postmortem fit.** `r4_L1\POSTMORTEM.md` is absent as of the audit date; fit is provisional. This direction preserves the network thesis while replacing correlation edges with ETF holdings and tests an economically interpretable flow shock, which addresses the concern that correlation records co-movement rather than transmission. Mechanism-first event tests address portfolio-first workflow. It still depends on sufficiently varied ETF links and may be weakened by the same narrow-universe and cost constraints that plausibly affected the prior strategy.

## Cross-direction decision risks

1. The three sources imply different conditional signs: informed retail flow may continue, while forced noninformational selling or ETF arbitrage may reverse. Pooling them into one generic pressure score would make the test hard to interpret and would reinstate the prior generic-factor problem.
2. Daily OHLCV cannot identify retail aggressor flow, mutual-fund forced demand, or ETF creations/redemptions. A candle-based substitute would not test these directions.
3. The L1 postmortem must be revisited when it appears. In particular, if L1’s root cause is data coverage, costs, stale constituents, or small-sample instability, the preferred direction may change for feasibility reasons, but its core hypothesis should not be rewritten to fit an outcome.
