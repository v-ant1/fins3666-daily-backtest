# R3 researcher: cycle 3 strategy ideas

## What changed after cycle 2

Cycle 2's `SKEW63_a08` is the strongest development candidate so far: it beat EW30 on Sharpe in 3/4 tests, had positive pooled excess, met the distinction and turnover criteria, but its pooled 10-session block-bootstrap p-value was 0.218. The earlier rule ranked assets by raw realized skewness. This cycle proposes a literature-standard **idiosyncratic skewness** score and a distinct **systematic co-skewness** score to check whether the result is tied to common market moves or asset-specific lottery-like tails; this is a measure refinement, not a sweep of raw-skew lookbacks. Cycle 2's ILLIQ63 and DOWNBETA63_Q25 both failed the Sharpe-count and pooled-excess criteria, so neither is repeated. The inverse-heat concentration in `r3_invheat/REPORT.md` also missed the goal and exceeded the turnover ceiling in one development test.

The proposal adds six unique parameterizations, two per family. The cumulative trial inventory is approximately 88 after cycle 2; use about **94** for DSR after these six are added, subject to coordinator reconciliation. All candidate lookbacks are at most 63 sessions, avoiding cycle 1's missing-history failure. This proposal uses only the Dow and cross-asset data. The sealed-holdout roster and prices were not accessed.

## Shared implementation and selection protocol

- **Inputs and timing:** Use adjusted daily OHLCV for the point-in-time Dow 30 roster or fixed cross-asset 30. Return-based signals use adjusted closes; gap signals use adjusted open, close and previous adjusted close; distribution signals use both the supplied split-adjusted `close` and dividend-adjusted `adjclose`. Compute signals after close using completed bars through that close and fill at the next eligible session's open. Require every specified observation; do not shorten lookbacks or impute missing data.
- **Weights:** Long-only and fully invested across all 30 names, with zero cash. Each strategy defines a desirability score with larger values meaning the asset should receive more weight. Sort desirability scores ascending with average ranks for ties, then `r_i=(average_rank_i-1)/29`. Set `w_i=(1/30)*(1+0.8*(2*r_i-1))`. Target weights are 0.67% to 6.00% without ties, and the rank formula gives about 20.7% target active share. No top-N concentration, leverage, or shorting.
- **Schedule, band and costs:** Rebalance every 21 eligible sessions on the validated engine's grid anchored at 2020-11-27. At each scheduled rebalance, compare the target vector with drifted pre-trade weights. If the sum of absolute weight changes is less than 1.0% of pre-trade NAV, skip the whole rebalance; otherwise reset to targets. Apply 0.05% per side to actual traded notional, including initial purchase and final liquidation. EW30 uses the same dates, next-open fills and costs.
- **Selection rule, separately for each family:** Record this rule before reading any cycle 3 selection results. Test the two listed variants for each family in each registered selection universe, 2020-11-27 to 2022-12-30. Exclude a variant if signal validation fails or mean actual turnover exceeds 50% per rebalance in either universe. Freeze within each family the eligible variant with the highest unweighted mean of Dow and cross-asset annualized daily net Sharpe; ties go to lower mean turnover and then the first listed variant. If neither variant qualifies, freeze EW30 for that family. Write all three frozen choices before any test-period result is opened; evaluate each frozen family once in each of the four registered development test periods. Do not change a choice after seeing a test. All six variants count toward the cumulative trials whether or not selected.
- **Report:** For each frozen direction, report after-cost net return, Sharpe, maximum drawdown, tracking error and information ratio versus same-universe EW30, active share, actual turnover, pooled block-bootstrap p-value and deflated Sharpe at the cumulative trial count. Verify criteria 1–5 from `specs/r3_goal.md`; a selection pass is not a test pass, and development results do not establish a sealed-holdout pass.

## Idea 1 — Separate idiosyncratic from systematic skewness

**Economic rationale.** Investor demand for lottery-like payoffs can raise the prices of positively skewed assets and lower their subsequent returns. Boyer, Mitton and Vorkink (2010) study expected idiosyncratic skewness; Harvey and Siddique (2000) distinguish systematic co-skewness in asset pricing. Cycle 2's raw realized skewness result motivates checking these standard components separately. Neither paper validates a 63-day estimator or an ETF cross-section, so this is a focused replication question, not a claim of a proven premium.

**Variant A — idiosyncratic skew (`IDIOSKEW63_a08`).** For each asset `i`, form daily adjusted close returns `r_i,d` and a leave-one-out equal-weight benchmark `m_i,d = mean(r_j,d for the other 29 assets)`. Using the 63 most recent completed return observations, estimate OLS with intercept `r_i,d = alpha_i + beta_i*m_i,d + e_i,d`. Compute `ISKEW_i = mean((e_i-mean(e_i))^3) / sd(e_i)^3` using population moments (`ddof=0`). The desirability score is `-ISKEW_i`: lower, more negative idiosyncratic skew receives more weight. Require nonzero residual standard deviation; otherwise the variant fails for that date/run. This residualizes only the common equal-weight return; it is not the Fama-French three-factor residual used in some studies.

**Variant B — systematic co-skew (`COSKEW63_a08`).** On the same 63 completed observations and leave-one-out benchmark, compute `COSKEW_i = mean((r_i-mean(r_i))*(m_i-mean(m_i))^2) / (sd(r_i)*sd(m_i)^2)` with population moments. The desirability score is `COSKEW_i`, so larger positive co-skew receives more weight as exposure associated with stronger returns on high-magnitude market days. Require both standard deviations to be positive. This variant is a distinct systematic-skew hypothesis, not a second raw-skew lookback; positive co-skew does not guarantee protection in a market crash.

**Selection.** Apply the shared per-family selection rule to these two variants; do not choose idiosyncratic versus systematic skew using any test period.

**Expected active risk, turnover and cost tolerance.** The target active share is about 20.7%; plausible annualized tracking error is 3–8%. A 63-session rolling estimate rebalanced every 21 sessions should generate around 15–35% actual turnover per rebalance, but rare tail observations may move ranks. Holding all 30 names and applying the existing 1% band should keep modeled costs below the 50% cap. Both measures require only daily returns and an equal-weight benchmark, so they apply to stocks and ETFs without asset-specific fundamentals. The ETF interpretation may be weaker because common market moves differ across rates, currencies, commodities and equities.

**Known failure mode.** Sixty-three observations give imprecise third moments, and residualization can make the score noisier rather than cleaner. The cycle 2 result may have been luck; neither measure may improve the pooled p-value. High coskew may also select assets that rise with broad market extremes but fail in a crash, while low idiosyncratic skew may select left-tail risk.

**Citations.** Boyer, B., Mitton, T. and Vorkink, K. (2010), “Expected Idiosyncratic Skewness,” *The Review of Financial Studies*, 23(1), 169–202. https://doi.org/10.1093/rfs/hhp041. Harvey, C. R. and Siddique, A. (2000), “Conditional Skewness in Asset Pricing Tests,” *The Journal of Finance*, 55(3), 1263–1295. https://doi.org/10.1111/0022-1082.00247.

## Idea 2 — Realized distribution income as a cross-asset carry proxy

**Economic rationale.** Cash distributions contribute to total returns and can be a rough yield or carry-like component for dividend stocks and distributing bond, property and other ETFs. Fama and French (1988) document a link between dividend yields and expected stock-market returns, but their aggregate long-horizon evidence does not validate a short-window cross-sectional sort. This idea uses only a realized distribution contribution that can be reconstructed from the supplied adjusted and unadjusted close series; it is explicitly **not** a futures-roll or forward-rate carry measure.

**Exact signal.** For each asset at close `t`, use `L` completed sessions to calculate `DIST_L = (adjclose_t / adjclose_(t-L)) - (close_t / close_(t-L))`. This is the difference between the adjusted total-return growth factor and the split-adjusted price growth factor; the common `-1` terms cancel. Annualize as `DIST_L * (252/L)` for reporting only; ranking is unchanged. Larger positive `DIST_L` means a larger realized dividend/distribution contribution and gets more weight. Do not include price momentum, volume, distribution announcements, or forecasts. If the data provider does not consistently adjust for distributions, fail the signal validation rather than substitute a price-only return.

**Variants and selection.** `DIST63_a08` uses `L=63, a=0.8`; `DIST63_a10` uses `L=63, a=1.0`. The signal is identical; the second variant tests a stronger, still long-only rank tilt (0–6.67% weights) as a portfolio-risk choice. Apply the common family selection rule. No other payout horizon or alpha value may be added.

**Expected active risk, turnover and cost tolerance.** Target active share is about 20.7% at `a=0.8` and 25.9% at `a=1.0`; expected annualized TE is around 3–8%. Distribution contributions change more slowly than daily returns, so 10–30% turnover per 21-session rebalance is plausible. The fully invested 30-name portfolio and slow rebalance schedule should keep modeled costs below 50% turnover in both universes. Both dividend-paying stocks and distributing ETFs have adjusted-close series, though non-distributing assets will naturally score near zero and ETF total-return adjustments can incorporate return-of-capital distributions.

**Known failure mode.** A 63-session sample can miss a quarterly or semiannual payment, and one ex-distribution date may dominate the score. Recent distributions can be cut or may be return of capital, so realized payout is not forward yield. Cross-asset ETFs include non-yielding assets; a payout tilt can become a narrow bond/property/equity-income sector bet and lose when price appreciation leads.

**Citation.** Fama, E. F. and French, K. R. (1988), “Dividend Yields and Expected Stock Returns,” *Journal of Financial Economics*, 22(1), 3–25. https://doi.org/10.1016/0304-405X(88)90020-7.

## Idea 3 — Tilt away from overnight gap risk

**Economic rationale.** The overnight and intraday portions of returns have different dynamics and can offset one another; Lou, Polk and Skouras (2019) document distinct overnight and intraday return continuation and reversal patterns. This candidate does not reuse a signed overnight-versus-intraday return forecast: it ranks assets by the share of realized variance occurring in overnight gaps, which cannot be traded continuously by a long-only daily strategy. It is a risk-management hypothesis, not an expected alpha claim.

**Inputs and exact measures.** For each asset and each of the prior 63 completed sessions, compute adjusted overnight gap `g_d = adjusted_open_d / adjusted_close_(d-1) - 1` and intraday return `u_d = adjusted_close_d / adjusted_open_d - 1`. Require positive prices and all observations. Define `GAPVOL63 = sample_sd(g_d)*sqrt(252)` and `GAPSHARE63 = sum(g_d^2) / (sum(g_d^2)+sum(u_d^2))`. The desirability scores are respectively `-GAPVOL63` and `-GAPSHARE63`, so lower absolute overnight risk or a lower overnight share of combined overnight and intraday squared returns gets more weight. If the denominator is zero, the share variant fails. These measures use no signal direction, volume, or pressure blend.

**Variants and selection.** `GAPVOL63_a08` uses desirability `-GAPVOL63`; `GAPSHARE63_a08` uses desirability `-GAPSHARE63`. Both use the common weight formula, 21-session schedule and band. Apply the shared per-family selection rule; do not modify thresholds after selection.

**Expected active risk, turnover and cost tolerance.** The rank tilt targets about 20.7% active share, with expected annualized TE around 3–8%. A rolling 63-session variance share should be relatively slow-moving, so 10–30% actual turnover per 21-session rebalance is plausible. Thirty-name diversification and next-open monthly-ish trades should tolerate the assumed 5 bp costs if the selection turnover cap is met. Open and close prices are present for both universes; the economic distinction is meaningful for stocks with overnight news gaps and international/commodity ETFs whose underlying markets operate in different time zones.

**Known failure mode.** A high gap share can reflect overnight information rather than avoidable execution risk; lowering it can sacrifice assets that earn compensation for bearing that risk. The cross-asset comparison is affected by each ETF's underlying market hours. `GAPVOL63` can collapse to another low-volatility sort, while `GAPSHARE63` could favor high intraday volatility; both should be compared with EW30 and reported without claiming they reduce total portfolio risk automatically. The earlier pressure blend included an intraday-minus-overnight component, so the distinction here is strictly variance-based risk allocation, not a new signed return signal.

**Citation.** Lou, D., Polk, C. and Skouras, S. (2019), “A Tug of War: Overnight versus Intraday Expected Returns,” *Journal of Financial Economics*, 134(1), 192–213. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jfineco.2019.03.011.
